\abstract{Closed-loop information extraction pools facts extracted by a language model in a knowledge base that scores each fact by its supporting evidence, and feeds that knowledge base back into the extractor. This paper analyses the conservative Noisy-OR aggregation rule with a mutual-exclusivity penalty that the DySECT system published for this purpose, and evaluates an independent implementation of the design. It proves the rule's boundedness, monotonicity and saturation, and a rival ceiling: for a single-valued predicate, a contested slot with m rival values scores below 1/(m+1), at most 0.5, so it cannot be admitted at any threshold of 0.5 or more. On two real-data snapshots the rule admitted no contested slots where a max-merge baseline admitted 6 and 40, and none under a structured source corrupted at up to 20\%. Counting distinct sources instead of repeated observations raised the precision of admitted facts by 0.03 to 0.11 in the product domain and by 0.05 to 0.29 on DocRED and BioRED, but only where the extractor repeats a fact within a source. A controlled, partly pre-registered evaluation found no end-to-end benefit from the loop's mechanisms: removing the feedback controller or the probabilistic knowledge base had no significant effect over thirty seeds, and leakage-free fine-tuning lowered per-product F1 on its primary metric. On DocRED and BioRED, aggregation lowered F1 for both extractors tested, because they recovered under 1.4\% of gold facts from more than one sentence: extractor recall, not the matching key, is the binding constraint.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models can extract relational facts from text without
task-specific training, but their output is noisy, and the same fact is
often extracted inconsistently across documents and runs. One response
is to close the loop between extraction and knowledge: store extracted
triples in a knowledge base that scores each fact by how much evidence
supports it, admit only well-supported facts, and feed the knowledge
base back into the extractor through prompting, feedback on missed and
spurious facts, and fine-tuning on the knowledge base's own
high-confidence contents. If it works, extraction improves the knowledge
base and the knowledge base improves extraction.

This design is not new. DySECT \citep{aminnaseri2026dysect} describes
such a closed loop. The loop evaluated in this paper follows it, and its
components are attributed to that work throughout: the architecture
(extraction into a probabilistically scored knowledge base, fed back
into extraction through prompt augmentation and fine-tuning on
knowledge-base-derived data); the aggregation rule, a conservative
Noisy-OR with a mutual-exclusivity penalty for single-valued predicates
(their Equations 1--2); its default shrinkage $\lambda=0.75$; and the
``Encouraging'' and ``Prohibitive'' names of its two feedback modes,
which this project's original design adopted for its own feedback
directions (Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}). This paper does not claim
any of these, or that such a system has not been built before.

What such a loop has not had is a controlled evaluation of whether its
mechanisms do what they are designed to do
(Section~\ref{sec:gap}). The published evaluation reports a recall gain
from knowledge-base-guided prompting on one benchmark, without a
module-level ablation, a multi-seed statistical protocol, a calibration
measurement, a held-out test separating the documents that build the
knowledge base from those that measure it, or proofs of the aggregation
rule's properties. This paper provides that evaluation for an
independent implementation of the design, on a synthetic product domain
and two public corpora, DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} and BioRED
\citep{luo2022biored}. It asks
four questions, each matching what was tested:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] What formal properties does the published
aggregation rule have, and can it admit a contested value for a
single-valued predicate, one for which rival values have also been
observed?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does counting distinct sources rather than repeated
observations improve the precision of what the rule admits, and under
what conditions does that correction act?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] In a controlled evaluation with held-out data,
multiple seeds, and pre-registered tests, do the loop's mechanisms --
the feedback controller, probabilistic aggregation, and fine-tuning on
knowledge-base-derived data -- improve extraction?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] On real text, does aggregation that requires
corroboration improve on no aggregation, and if not, what limits it?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The paper makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] A formal analysis of the published conservative Noisy-OR
aggregation rule, including the \emph{rival-ceiling} result: for a
single-valued predicate, any contested slot scores below
$1/(m+1) \le 0.5$, where $m \ge 1$ is the number of rival values, and so
cannot be admitted at any threshold at or above $0.5$
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}).
\item[(ii)] An empirical demonstration that the ceiling blocks contested
slots: on two real-data snapshots the published rule admitted none,
against 6 and 40 for a max-merge baseline, and it admitted none under a
structured source corrupted at controlled rates of 5\%, 10\%, and 20\%
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}
and~\ref{sec:results-provenance}).
\item[(iii)] A corrected rule based on distinct-source counting, with
measured precision gains -- on the product domain at the operating
threshold, and on DocRED and BioRED -- and an explicit scope limit: it
acts only when the extractor repeats a fact within a single source, and
had no measurable effect for an extractor that rarely did
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical},
\ref{sec:results-docred}, and~\ref{sec:results-biored}).
\item[(iv)] An independent controlled evaluation of the closed-loop
design, finding no end-to-end benefit from its mechanisms. Removing the
feedback controller or the probabilistic knowledge base had no
significant effect at thirty seeds; a leakage-free fine-tuning
evaluation is negative on its pre-registered primary metric; and on
DocRED and BioRED, with two extractors, aggregation lowered $F_1$
relative to no aggregation. The binding constraint on aggregation over
real text is extractor recall, not the key used to match facts: the
extractors recovered only 0.26\% (DocRED) and 0.75--1.35\% (BioRED) of
gold facts from more than one sentence, although 50.2\% of DocRED's
gold facts have two or more evidence sentences and 35.1\% of BioRED's
are co-mentioned in two or more sentences
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-ablation}, \ref{sec:results-finetuning},
\ref{sec:results-docred}, and~\ref{sec:results-biored}).
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related work, including DySECT
in detail, and Section~\ref{sec:gap} states the evaluation gap this
paper addresses. Section~\ref{sec:framework} describes the evaluated

Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Background}

\paragraph{Extraction.} Open information extraction derives relational
triples without predefined relation types
\citep{banko2007openie,liu2024openiesurvey}, while document-level and
sentence-level benchmarks such as DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} and TACRED
\citep{zhang2017tacred} standardised schema-guided evaluation. Generative
extractors cast extraction as sequence generation, from REBEL
\citep{cabot2021rebel} and GenIE \citep{josifoski2022genie} to TANL
\citep{paolini2021tanl}; universal frameworks unify extraction tasks under
one model \citep{lu2022uie,wang2023instructuie}; and joint, open-world,
and zero-shot methods broaden coverage
\citep{wadden2019dygiepp,zhang2022oamine,levy2017zeroshot}; all treat
extraction as a terminal step.

\paragraph{Knowledge and retrieval.} Retrieval-augmented generation
grounds generation in retrieved evidence
\citep{lewis2020rag,guu2020realm,asai2023selfrag}, including graph-based
memories \citep{gutierrez2024hipporag,edge2025graphrag}, only at
inference time. Knowledge-graph population work treats confidence and
uncertainty as open problems \citep{bach2017psl,ji2021kgsurvey,nguyen2020kgcsurvey},
and attribution methods are largely post hoc
\citep{ribeiro2016lime,zhao2023explainability}.

\paragraph{Adaptation.} Parameter-efficient fine-tuning reduces the cost
of adapting pretrained models \citep{devlin2019bert,houlsby2019peft,
lester2021prompttuning,hu2021lora,dettmers2023qlora}, and instruction and
preference tuning broaden what fine-tuning teaches
\citep{wei2022flan,ouyang2022instructgpt,rafailov2023dpo}, all from
curated supervision. Self-improvement methods generate their own
supervision
\citep{madaan2023selfrefine,gulcehre2023rest,chen2024spin,wang2023selfinstruct,schick2023toolformer},
and continual learning addresses the stability of sequential adaptation
\citep{wang2024continuallearning,li2017lwf}; none maintains a persistent
structured knowledge base as the source of supervision. AutoML automates
model selection and search
\citep{hutter2019automl,he2021automlsurvey,feurer2015autosklearn,feurer2022autosklearn2,erickson2020autogluon,das2020sagemaker,elsken2019nas}
but treats data extraction as a fixed upstream step.

\subsection{Closest Prior System: DySECT}
\label{sec:related-work-dysect}

DySECT \citep{aminnaseri2026dysect} populates a self-expanding knowledge
base with LLM-extracted triples, scores them with a probabilistic
confidence and reasoning layer built on Theo \citep{mitchell1989theo},
and feeds the knowledge base back into the extractor through prompt
tuning, few-shot sampling, or fine-tuning on knowledge-base-derived
synthetic data. On DocRED it reports recall gains of 5--8 percentage
points at the first knowledge-base-guided iteration for all four models
it evaluates (GPT-4.1: 22.80\%$\to$30.62\%; GPT-4.1-mini:
12.72\%$\to$20.22\%; Kimi~K2.5: 32.03\%$\to$37.85\%; LLaMA-3.3~70B:
23.23\%$\to$28.78\%), and its knowledge base can be inspected and
curated by a user.

\paragraph{What this paper takes from DySECT.} The aggregation rule
analysed here is DySECT's: its Equations 1--2 define
$C_{\mathrm{agg}}(t) = 1 - \prod_i (1-\lambda c_i)^{f_i}$ with default
shrinkage $\lambda=0.75$, followed by the mutual-exclusivity penalty
$C(t) = C_{\mathrm{agg}}(t)/(m(t)+1)$, which is the rule evaluated
throughout this paper (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}). DySECT
names its two prompt-feedback modes (its Section 4): an
\emph{Encouraging} (positive) mode, which injects high-confidence
sub-concepts and representative relations as positive examples to
encourage extraction of further instances of similar concept families,
and a \emph{Prohibitive} (negative) mode, which marks well-covered or
saturated sub-concepts as already extracted so that the model avoids
redundant patterns. The original design of the implementation evaluated
here adopted both names, and it also specified the same KNN-based
clustering over embeddings that DySECT uses to build its concept
hierarchy (its Section 2.2). The names are therefore DySECT's, not this
project's coinage. The implemented feedback controller does not use them
in code, and its false-pattern block, which flags accepted facts known
to be wrong, differs in purpose from DySECT's Prohibitive mode, which
discourages redundant re-extraction (Section~\ref{sec:framework-feedback}).
The closed loop, the probabilistic confidence layer, prompt-augmentation
feedback, and fine-tuning on knowledge-base-derived data are likewise
DySECT's design. This paper claims none of them; its contributions are
the formal analysis of the rule and the evaluation.

\paragraph{Differences.} DySECT describes two capabilities that the
evaluated implementation does not have, KNN-based hierarchical concept
abstraction and a human curation interface
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-gaps}). Conversely, the evaluated
implementation seeds the knowledge base from structured records as well
as text, and gates fine-tuning data on structured corroboration
(Section~\ref{sec:framework}); DySECT describes neither. The two
evaluations use different benchmarks, models, and metrics, so no direct
numerical comparison is made.

\subsection{Evaluation Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

A closed loop of this kind therefore already exists. What its published
evaluation does not include is a module-level ablation isolating which
component (aggregation, feedback, fine-tuning) is responsible for a
change; a multi-seed protocol separating real effects from run-to-run
variance; a calibration measurement of the scores the aggregation rule
produces; a held-out test separating the documents that build the
knowledge base from those that measure it; and proofs of the rule's
properties. This paper supplies each of these for an independent
implementation (Sections~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}--\ref{sec:results}).
It does not assess DySECT's hierarchical abstraction or curation
interface, which the evaluated implementation lacks.

\subsection{Dual-Source Extraction}
\label{sec:framework-extraction}

For the product domain, a structured path
(\texttt{src/extractors/structured.py}) maps record fields to the 13
predicates deterministically, at a fixed confidence of $0.98$, and an
unstructured path (\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_llm.py}) prompts a
pinned LLM at temperature 0, which self-reports a confidence in
$[0.80, 0.96]$. Both write to one triple schema. A prompt-parameterised
extractor (\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_openie.py}) serves CaRB,
DocRED, and BioRED. The fine-tuning data-generation and evaluation
prompt (\texttt{src/prompts.py}) uses a different self-reported range,
$[0.85, 0.99]$ (Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}). Each pipeline
reads its own fixed input format (records for the product domain and
CaRB, JSON for DocRED, PubTator for BioRED); there is no general
ingestion module and no PDF, DOCX, or SQL support.
% [src/datasets/*.py; verified by direct search of src/ and scripts/]

\subsection{Probabilistic Knowledge Base}
\label{sec:framework-pkb}

Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict} are implemented in
\texttt{src/pkb\_math.py} and applied to each candidate by
\texttt{src/pkb\_instrumentation.py}.
\texttt{src/probkb\_v2\_adapter.py} keeps every accepted observation; no
code path deletes an accepted candidate, so the knowledge base grows
monotonically. A max-merge adapter
(\texttt{src/deterministic\_kb\_adapter.py}) implements the same
interface for the ablation (Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}), and
\texttt{src/pkb\_replay.py} reconstructs any saved end-of-iteration state
from stored observations, used for the closed-loop test and the offline
rule comparison (Sections~\ref{sec:results-closedloop}
and~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}).

\subsection{Feedback Controller}
\label{sec:framework-feedback}

\texttt{src/feedback\_builder.py} builds the hint that guides
re-extraction from three blocks: missing facts (gold keys not yet
accepted), false patterns (accepted keys not in gold), and
under-covered predicates (fewer than two accepted observations). Every
call site passes it the \emph{training-split gold labels}
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py; src/experiment_runner.py]
. The feedback is therefore computed against known answers, not against
any uncertainty or disagreement internal to the knowledge base, so the
loop is not self-supervised: it presupposes labelled training data,
which exists here only because the domain is synthetic
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The original design named these
directions after DySECT's ``Encouraging'' and ``Prohibitive'' modes, but
those labels do not appear in the code, and the false-pattern block
differs in purpose from DySECT's Prohibitive mode
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}).

\subsection{Synthetic Supervision, Provenance Filtering, and Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:framework-finetuning}

Synthetic fine-tuning examples are built from the knowledge base's
above-threshold triples, grouped per product into the paragraph-in,
JSON-out shape used for evaluation
(\texttt{scripts/dec006\_regenerate\_synth\_data.py}). By default a
triple is included only if at least one of its observations comes from
the structured source (\texttt{src/provenance\_filter.py};
Section~\ref{sec:results-provenance}). Fine-tuning
(\texttt{scripts/dec006\_lora\_finetune.py}; QLoRA on
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) is started manually on a rented GPU; no code
triggers it automatically from recall or knowledge-base growth, and
there is no adapter registry or rollback.
% [grep for registry/rollback/auto_trigger across src/ and scripts/: none]

\subsection{What Was Specified but Not Implemented}
\label{sec:framework-gaps}

Six elements of the original design have no code in the evaluated
implementation: KNN-based hierarchical concept abstraction;
automatically triggered fine-tuning; an adapter registry with rollback;
character-level source offsets (provenance is stored as a truncated text
snippet); a human validation interface; and ingestion beyond CSV, JSON,
and plain text. They are scope limits of what this paper evaluates
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Formal Analysis of the Aggregation Rule}
\label{sec:formal-analysis}

\subsection{Definition and Attribution}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-definition}

The aggregation rule is \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s (their
Equations 1--2), with their default shrinkage
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}); this paper cites it rather
than claiming it. For a slot with candidate object $t$ supported by
observations with self-reported confidences
$c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,1]$ and shrinkage $\lambda \in (0,1]$ (default
$\lambda=0.75$)
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py; matches aminnaseri2026dysect Eq. 1]
, the \emph{conservative Noisy-OR support} is
\begin{equation}
A(t) \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(1 - \lambda c_i\bigr).
\label{eq:noisyor}
\end{equation}
For a slot designated functionally single-valued, with $m(t)$ competing
object values already accepted for the same (subject, predicate) slot,
the admitted confidence is
\begin{equation}
C(t) \;=\; \frac{A(t)}{m(t) + 1}.
\label{eq:conflict}
\end{equation}
For a non-functional predicate (only \texttt{has\_color} in the product
domain), $C(t) = A(t)$. A candidate is admitted when $C(t) \ge \tau$,
with $\tau=0.88$ in the product domain
% [configs/functional_predicates_product_domain.json; scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py]
. This section proves properties stated but not proved in
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}, and tests offline whether a corrected rule
outperforms the published one.

\subsection{Formal Properties}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-properties}

\begin{proposition}[Range and boundedness]
For any non-empty finite multiset of confidences $c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,1]$
and $\lambda \in (0,1]$, $A(t) \in [0,1)$ whenever at least one $c_i < 1$,
and $A(t) \to 1$ only in the limit $n\to\infty$ with every $c_i$ bounded
away from $0$. Consequently $C(t) \in [0,1)$ for every functionally
single-valued slot with $m(t) \ge 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each factor $(1-\lambda c_i) \in (0,1]$ since $\lambda c_i \in [0,1]$, so
the product $\prod_i(1-\lambda c_i) \in (0,1]$ and is strictly positive
for finite $n$; hence $A(t) = 1 - \prod_i(1-\lambda c_i) < 1$ strictly for
every finite observation set. $A(t) \ge 0$ follows since each factor is
$\le 1$, so the product is $\le 1$. Dividing by $m(t)+1 \ge 1$ preserves
$C(t) \in [0,1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Order-invariance]
$A(t)$ depends only on the multiset $\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}$, not on the order
in which observations are accumulated.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication is commutative, so $\prod_i(1-\lambda c_i)$ is invariant
under any permutation of the index set $i=1,\dots,n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity in evidence]
\label{prop:monotone}
Adding an observation with confidence $c_{n+1} > 0$ to an existing set of
$n$ observations strictly increases $A(t)$; $A(t)$ is non-decreasing in
$n$ and strictly increasing whenever every added observation has
$c_i > 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $A_n = 1-\prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i)$. Then
$A_{n+1} - A_n = \prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i) \cdot \lambda c_{n+1} > 0$
whenever $c_{n+1} > 0$, since $\prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i) > 0$ by the
boundedness proposition above.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Saturation under repeated observation]
\label{prop:saturation}
If the same confidence $c$ is observed $n$ times, $A_n = 1-(1-\lambda c)^n
\to 1$ as $n \to \infty$, geometrically, at rate $(1-\lambda c)$: the
residual $1 - A_n = (1-\lambda c)^n$ decreases by the constant factor
$(1-\lambda c)$ with every additional identical observation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} with $c_i=c$ for all $i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Corroboration requirement]
\label{prop:corroboration}
With $\lambda=0.75$ and $\tau=0.88$, no single observation, regardless of
its confidence, is sufficient for admission: a triple requires at least
two corroborating observations to reach $\tau$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A single observation with the maximum possible confidence $c_1=1$ yields
$A(t) = \lambda = 0.75 < \tau = 0.88$ from Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} with
$n=1$. More generally, admission with $n$ observations each at $c=1$
requires $1-(1-\lambda)^n \ge \tau$, i.e.\ $n \ge \ln(1-\tau)/\ln(1-\lambda)
= \ln(0.12)/\ln(0.25) \approx 1.53$, so $n \ge 2$.
\end{proof}
For example, a fact supported only by one structured observation (fixed
confidence $0.98$) scores $A(t) = 0.735$, below $\tau$, however
trustworthy that source is.
% [EVID-041; src/extractors/structured.py]

\begin{proposition}[Conflict-penalty properties]
\label{prop:conflict}
For fixed $A(t)$, $C(t) = A(t)/(m(t)+1)$ is strictly decreasing in
$m(t)$, and $C(t) = A(t)$ exactly when $m(t)=0$ (no penalty when the slot
is uncontested).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\partial C(t)/\partial m(t) = -A(t)/(m(t)+1)^2 < 0$ for $A(t) > 0$, and
$C(t)|_{m(t)=0} = A(t)/1 = A(t)$ directly from Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rival ceiling]
\label{prop:ceiling}
For any functionally single-valued predicate slot with $m(t) \ge 1$
competing object values, $C(t) < 1/(m(t)+1) \le 0.5$, independent of how
much observational support $A(t)$ accumulates.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the boundedness proposition, $A(t) < 1$ for any finite observation
set. Hence $C(t) = A(t)/(m(t)+1) < 1/(m(t)+1)$. For $m(t) \ge 1$,
$1/(m(t)+1) \le 1/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:ceiling-consequence}
With $\tau = 0.88 > 0.5$, a contested functionally single-valued slot
($m(t) \ge 1$) can never be admitted under
Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict}, regardless of how many
corroborating observations accumulate for the leading candidate. Since
the implementation never deletes an accepted candidate
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-pkb}), a contested functional slot is not
merely slow to resolve: it is structurally unresolvable at $\tau=0.88$.
\end{corollary}

The ceiling depends only on the algebraic form of
Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}, not on the domain, the extractor, or the
amount of evidence, so it holds for any deployment of the rule with
$\tau \ge 0.5$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}).

\begin{proposition}[Independence assumption and repeated observation]
\label{prop:independence}
Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} is a Noisy-OR combination rule, which assumes
each $c_i$ is drawn from a conditionally independent evidence source.
This assumption is violated when the same extractor, invoked at
temperature $0$, is asked to re-read the same source text: the output is
deterministic, so repeated invocations produce byte-identical
observations that are treated by Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} as though they
were independent corroboration, inflating $A(t)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}'s saturation dynamic regardless of
whether the underlying claim is correct.
\end{proposition}
The violation is observable in the data: in the 200-product snapshot, 31
unstructured-only triples were observed 2--21 times each by this
mechanism, and all 31 were incorrect
% [EVID-029]
. The same rule's scores are poorly calibrated on real data (expected
calibration error $0.333$; Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration}).
The computational cost of the implementation is analysed in
Supplementary Section~S3.

\subsection{Empirical Validation: The Ceiling and Tested Corrections}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}

Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling-consequence} says the ceiling must block every
contested single-valued slot, but not whether such slots arise on real
data or whether a corrected rule does better. Both were tested offline
(DEC-026) by replaying the stored observations of two independent
gold-labelled snapshots, 657 triples from 35 products (dataset A) and
2,241 triples from 140 products (dataset B), through six rules applied
to identical observations: R1, max-merge ($C(t)=\max_i c_i$, no conflict
handling); R2, the published rule; R3, distinct-source counting
(below); R4, an evidence-share reformulation
$C(t)=A(t)^2/\sum_j A(t_j)$ over the candidates $j$ of a slot; R5, R3 and
R4 combined; and R6, the unsquared share $A(t)/\sum_j A(t_j)$. Each rule
is evaluated at its own validation-selected threshold and at the fixed
$\tau=0.88$, on held-out subjects (35 and 92), with 95\% confidence
intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples over subjects. The full
per-rule tables are in Supplementary Section~S2.
% [EVID-041; EVID-013/014/036 (dataset A); EVID-029/030 (dataset B)]

\paragraph{The ceiling is confirmed.} R2 admits no contested
single-valued slot on either dataset at either threshold. Max-merge,
evaluated identically, admits 6 and 40 at its selected thresholds and
20 and 69 at $\tau=0.88$. It is therefore the ceiling, not an absence of
contested slots, that accounts for R2's zero. Max-merge's higher $F_1$
at its own selected threshold does not survive at $\tau=0.88$, where its
significant precision loss offsets its significant recall gain
(Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}).
% [EVID-041]

\paragraph{Two corrections that do not help.} R4 removes the algebraic
ceiling and reduces correctly to $C(t)=A(t)$ when $m(t)=0$, but its
admitted set is identical to R2's on both datasets ($F_1$ difference
CI exactly $[0,0]$), because no observed contested-slot score comes
near the threshold (maximum $0.581$ against a selected $\tau=0.73$ on
dataset A). R6, tested afterwards as an exploratory check, degenerates
to $C(t)=1$ for every uncontested slot (495 of 495 such rows in dataset
A), and its $F_1$ is significantly below R2's on dataset A (95\% CI
$[-0.087, -0.012]$) and not significantly different on B. Any
share-based replacement for Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} must reduce to
$A(t)$ when $m(t)=0$ to avoid this degeneracy; R4 does and R6 does not.
Neither is claimed as a contribution.
% [EVID-041]

\paragraph{The corrected rule: distinct-source counting (R3).} R3
targets the independence violation of
Proposition~\ref{prop:independence} rather than the ceiling: it applies
Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} over distinct
$(\text{source\_type}, \text{source\_id})$ pairs, keeping each source's
maximum confidence, and leaves Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} unchanged. At
$\tau=0.88$ it raises precision significantly on both datasets at
exactly unchanged recall, and raises $F_1$ significantly
(Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}). It also raises $F_1$ at each
dataset's own selected threshold ($+0.0106$, 95\% CI $[0.0028,0.0222]$;
$+0.0039$, $[0.0013,0.0075]$), so the gain does not depend on threshold
selection. Its effect outside the product domain is reported in
Sections~\ref{sec:results-docred} and~\ref{sec:results-biored}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental-setup}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:setup-datasets}

\paragraph{Synthetic product domain.} The primary domain is a
deterministic synthetic consumer-product generator
(\texttt{src/datasets/product\_generator.py}, \texttt{random.seed(42)})
over a fixed 13-predicate schema (\texttt{manufactured\_by},
\texttt{belongs\_to\_category}, \texttt{has\_price\_usd},
\texttt{has\_screen\_size\_inch}, \texttt{has\_ram\_gb},
\texttt{has\_storage\_gb}, \texttt{has\_battery\_life\_hours},
\texttt{has\_weight\_kg}, \texttt{has\_color}, \texttt{made\_of\_material},
\texttt{target\_market\_region}, \texttt{supports\_fast\_charging},
\texttt{has\_noise\_cancellation}). For each product it draws brand,
category, colour, material, region, and eight numeric attributes from
fixed pools, and produces (i) a structured record with all 13 facts,
(ii) a text paragraph verbalising a random 7 of the 13, and (iii) two
gold sets: \texttt{gold\_structured} (all 13 facts) and
\texttt{gold\_unstructured} (the facts verbalised in the text).
\textbf{The structured records and the gold triples come from the same
generator call}, which matters for the provenance filter
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
% [src/datasets/product_generator.py]

Two sizes are used, both split 70/10/20 (train/validation/test) with
\texttt{random.Random(7)}, a seed distinct from the generation seed
(\texttt{src/leakage\_split.py}): 50 products (35/5/10;
\texttt{data/product\_split.csv}) and 200 products (140/20/40;
\texttt{data/product\_split\_200.csv}). The same leakage rules apply to
every experiment. Test products are evaluated on text extraction only
and are never seeded with their own structured facts, and they are
never used for synthetic training data, fine-tuning, few-shot examples,
feedback exemplars, or threshold selection. Validation products may be
used for threshold and hyperparameter selection. Training products may
seed the knowledge base and generate synthetic supervision.

\paragraph{Public benchmarks.} \textbf{CaRB} \citep{bhardwaj2019carb}:
548 of the 641 test sentences, those whose text matches between the
sentence and gold-annotation files (Supplementary Section~S1).
% [EVID-035]
\textbf{DocRED} \citep{yao2019docred}: the 845 documents of the
998-document human-annotated development split (\texttt{thunlp/DocRED})
that contain at least one gold fact supported by two or more evidence
sentences, with 6,861 sentences and 11,344 entity-level gold facts, of
which 5,691 (50.2\%) have two or more evidence sentences. The extractor
is run on every sentence.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-031; EVID-046]
An earlier 15-document pilot sent only the gold evidence sentences to
the extractor, an oracle setting that tells it which sentences contain
a relation; it is superseded.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020]
\textbf{BioRED} \citep{luo2022biored}: all 500 abstracts of the Train
(400) and Dev (100) splits (PubTator format); the Test split is not
used. They contain 5,462 sentences (the title plus the abstract, split
by a fixed rule) and 4,906 gold facts, each an unordered pair of
annotated concepts with one of eight relation types. BioRED has no
evidence-sentence annotations; as a proxy, 35.1\% of gold facts have
both concepts mentioned together in two or more sentences, and 89.7\%
in at least one.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032; EVID-047]
An earlier 15-abstract pilot sent each whole abstract in one call, so
every document had one source and nothing could be aggregated; it
tested the extractor only and is superseded.
% [EVID-024; EVID-047]

\subsection{Models and Hardware}
\label{sec:setup-models}

The pipeline's extractor, used for every product-domain, DocRED, and
BioRED run, is \texttt{meta-llama/llama-3.1-8b-instruct}
\citep{grattafiori2024llama3} via OpenRouter at temperature $0$.
% [EVID-013; src/extractors/openrouter_llm.py]
On BioRED it is compared with \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-v3.2}
\citep{deepseekai2025deepseekv32} as an external baseline, given the
same prompts and inputs.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032]
On CaRB it is compared with \texttt{deepseek/deepseek-v3.2} on all 548
sentences, and with \texttt{openai/gpt-4o} \citep{openai2024gpt4o},
\texttt{anthropic/claude-sonnet-5} \citep{anthropic2026claudesonnet5},
and \texttt{google/gemini-2.5-pro} \citep{comanici2025gemini25} on the
30-sentence pilot only (Supplementary Section~S1).
% [EVID-032; EVID-035]
The fine-tuning base model is \texttt{mistralai/Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3}
\citep{jiang2023mistral}, adapted with QLoRA: rank 16, $\alpha=32$,
dropout 0.05, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$, batch size 2 with gradient
accumulation 4, bf16.
% [EVID-026]
The leakage-free evaluation selected 8 epochs on its validation split
from $\{2,3,5,8\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning}).
% [EVID-044]
Training and adapter evaluation ran on rented RTX 4090 and RTX 3090 GPUs.

\subsection{Hyperparameters and Prompts}
\label{sec:setup-hyperparameters}

The product domain uses shrinkage $\lambda=0.75$ and admission threshold
$\tau=0.88$, the default \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} report.
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py; scripts/dec006_scaleup_probkb_run.py]
DocRED and BioRED use $\lambda=0.5$ and $\tau=0.70$, so that two
corroborating observations suffice for admission (Supplementary
Section~S1), and no conflict penalty, because no functional-predicate
policy is defined for them
(\texttt{configs/docred\_functional\_predicates.json} is empty).
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020, DEC-032]
The product-domain loop runs four extraction iterations on training
products: structured facts seed the knowledge base at iteration 1 only,
and the LLM re-extracts at every iteration, guided from iteration 2 by
the feedback hint. Validation and test products receive one
text-only extraction pass, guided only by the trained knowledge base's
locked context.
% [EVID-013]
DocRED and BioRED documents are processed once, with one extraction call
per sentence; the same fact extracted from several sentences supplies
the repeated observations.

Confidence is self-reported by the model in response to a prompt
instruction, not derived from token probabilities or calibrated
% [EVID-041]
. The pipeline's extraction prompt (\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_llm.py})
asks for a confidence between $0.80$ and $0.96$; the fine-tuning
data-generation and evaluation prompt (\texttt{src/prompts.py}, shared by
both so the tasks match) asks for one between $0.85$ and $0.99$. The two
ranges are deliberately different and are not comparable. CaRB, DocRED,
and BioRED use their own prompts (\texttt{prompts/openie\_carb\_v1.txt},
\texttt{openie\_docred\_v1.txt}, and \texttt{openie\_biored\_sent\_v1.txt}, a
per-sentence version of the BioRED pilot's prompt listing all eight
relation types where the pilot listed six); CaRB's is open-phrase, and
the others enumerate their closed relation sets.
% [EVID-021; Decision log.md, DEC-020, DEC-032]

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:setup-metrics}

Unless stated otherwise, triples are scored by normalised exact-match
set comparison (\texttt{src/evaluator.py}: lower-cased and
whitespace-normalised subject, predicate, and object, intersected as
sets). \textbf{CaRB} is reported under the official scorer (lenient span
matching; AUC and an optimal precision/recall/$F_1$ point), which this
paper cites; the internal exact-match evaluator is known to undercount
matches the official scorer accepts.
% [EVID-031; EVID-035]
\textbf{DocRED} is scored per document against gold entities rather than
strings: a predicted triple matches a gold fact when its relation name
matches and its subject and object, after the normalisation defined in
Section~\ref{sec:results-docred}, equal the normalised form of any gold
mention of the head and tail entities. Predictions are deduplicated per
gold fact, and the same evaluator is used for every aggregation arm.
\textbf{BioRED} is scored the same way against gold concepts, matching
either argument order. For both, exact matching against each entity's
first mention (and, for BioRED, a relaxed containment match) is a
secondary measure, as in the earlier pilots.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-031, DEC-032; EVID-046; EVID-047]

\paragraph{Recall denominators.} Recall is $TP/(TP+FN)$, and its gold
set is named wherever it appears. The offline rule comparison
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}) uses
\texttt{gold\_unstructured} facts of training products; the closed-loop
test uses the larger \texttt{gold\_structured} set; and held-out
extraction recall (no admission step) is measured on other products.
These figures are not comparable with one another (Supplementary
Section~S1).
% [EVID-041]

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:setup-statistics}

\paragraph{Multi-seed comparisons.} The fine-tuning results and the
$N=20$ ablation use five seeds ($42$--$46$); the $N=50$ ablation uses
thirty ($42$--$71$). We report the mean and sample standard deviation
($\text{ddof}=1$) across seeds, a one-sample $t$-test against a fixed
base-model score, and a paired $t$-test with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test
between configurations evaluated on the same seeds. At five seeds the
Wilcoxon test cannot report $p<0.0625$ (Supplementary Section~S1).
% [scripts/dec006_5seed_stats.py; EVID-020; EVID-039; EVID-044; EVID-045]

\paragraph{Minimum detectable effect.} For every null result we report
the smallest effect a paired $t$-test could detect with 80\% power at
$\alpha=0.05$, with the required Cohen's $d$ solved from the noncentral
$t$ distribution, using both the ablated configuration's standard
deviation and that of the paired differences.
% [scripts/dec029_analyze.py]

\paragraph{Bootstrap confidence intervals.} The offline rule comparison
uses a subject-level cluster bootstrap: 10,000 resamples of test-split
subjects with replacement, summing each subject's true/false
positive/negative counts before recomputing precision, recall, and
$F_1$, with the $[2.5, 97.5]$ percentile interval as the 95\% CI.
% [EVID-041; scripts/dec026_aggregation_rules_replay.py]
The leakage-free fine-tuning evaluation uses a paired bootstrap over its
40 test products (mean per-product $F_1$ difference, fine-tuned minus
base, averaged over five seeds). The DocRED and BioRED runs use a paired
bootstrap over documents.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-027, DEC-031, DEC-032]

\paragraph{Pre-registration and nulls.} The leakage-free fine-tuning
evaluation, the thirty-seed ablation, and the DocRED and BioRED runs
were pre-registered, with primary metrics and decision rules fixed
before any result was seen. A result with $p \ge 0.05$, or a bootstrap
CI including zero, is reported as a null exactly as found, without
re-running, adding seeds, or post-hoc adjustment.
% [Decision log.md, DEC-027, DEC-029, DEC-031, DEC-032; README.md ground rule 5]

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why Aggregation Fails on DocRED and BioRED, and the Divergence from DySECT}
\label{sec:discussion-docred}

An earlier account in this project attributed the DocRED result, on a
15-document pilot, to exact-string matching: the same fact phrased
differently in different sentences would never share a key. The
845-document run tested that account and does not support it. A
deployable normalised key closed none of the gap to no aggregation, and
the oracle key closed 7.3\%, although it already pooled 28 of the 29 gold
facts that the extractions recovered from more than one sentence
(Section~\ref{sec:results-docred}).
% [EVID-046]

The failure comes instead from the scarcity of cross-sentence
corroboration in what the extractor produces. Half of DocRED's gold
facts have two or more supporting sentences, but the sentence-level
extractor recovered only 645 of 11,344 facts at all and 29 from two or
more sentences. A rule that admits a fact only when it is corroborated
cannot admit facts that are never corroborated, whatever the key
(Proposition~\ref{prop:corroboration}). The binding constraint is
therefore extractor recall, compounded across sentences. The same
pattern holds on BioRED, with different text, a different schema, and an
external baseline (Section~\ref{sec:results-biored}): a third of its gold
facts are co-mentioned in two or more sentences, but the extractors
recovered only 0.75\% and 1.35\% of facts from more than one sentence.
The stronger extractor recovered significantly more corroborated facts,
but far too few, and it lost more $F_1$ to aggregation only because its
unaggregated output was more precise while both aggregated outputs ended
near the same low $F_1$. In the product domain, by contrast, the same
fact is extracted repeatedly across iterations and sources, so the
constraint does not bind. The aggregation stage therefore adds value only
when the extractor recovers the same fact from more than one source often
enough; a stronger or document-level extractor would move this boundary.
% [EVID-046; EVID-047]

\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} report a recall \emph{improvement} from
knowledge-base-guided extraction on DocRED for all four models they
evaluate. The two results are not a controlled comparison, and two
differences, neither tested here, may explain the opposite direction.
First, their extractors (GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, LLaMA-3.3~70B, and
Kimi~K2.5) are all larger than the Llama-3.1-8B used here, and the
finding above suggests extractor recall matters most. Second, their
recall is extraction recall under knowledge-base-guided
\emph{prompting}, whereas this paper measures recall after a
confidence-threshold \emph{admission} step, so the two figures may not
measure the same quantity.

\subsection{Why the Repeat-Counting Correction Succeeds Where Evidence-Share Does Not}
\label{sec:discussion-r3}

R3 is the one tested correction with a robust gain over R2 at the fixed
threshold (Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}); R4 is an exact null
and R6 is negative (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}). An
account consistent with all three is that they target different
defects. Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} treats every repeated observation as
independent evidence, but repeated readings of the same source by one
extractor at temperature 0 are deterministic repeats
(Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}). R3 corrects this at the
\emph{input} to the aggregation, by collapsing repeats from the same
source. R4 and R6 rescale the \emph{output} of the already over-counted
$A(t)$, changing how a contested slot's confidence is divided but not
how often a repeat is counted. R6 shows that rescaling the output can
also hurt: because $A(t)/A(t)=1$ for any uncontested slot, it collapses
to a constant exactly where the input carries information. This account
fits the measured pattern but was not tested against alternatives.

The same defect appears outside the product domain, and it defines R3's
scope. On DocRED the extractor emitted the same triple more than once
from a single sentence 154 times (exact key), and R3 raised the precision
of admitted triples from $0.060$ to $0.250$, on too few triples to change
$F_1$. On BioRED, Llama-3.1-8B repeated itself 138 times and R3 raised
its precision by $0.054$; DeepSeek-V3.2 did so 6 times and R3 had no
measurable effect. Where the discarded repeats include correct facts,
R3 can cost recall, as with Llama-3.1-8B on BioRED. R3 is therefore a
correction for extractors that repeat themselves within a source, not a
general improvement to aggregation.
% [EVID-046; EVID-047]

\subsection{What the Ablations Do and Do Not Show}
\label{sec:discussion-ablations}

Two results bear on whether the Noisy-OR PKB helps. The thirty-seed
ablation found no significant difference between the full pipeline and a
max-merge variant ($+0.034$ for max-merge, 95\% CI $[-0.010, 0.082]$).
Its detectable effect is about $0.07$--$0.09$ absolute $F_1$, against
about $0.29$ at five seeds, so an effect of that size in the PKB's favour
is ruled out, a smaller one is not, and the point estimate points the
other way. Separately, in the offline replay max-merge exceeded the
published rule at its own selected threshold but not at the shared
$\tau=0.88$, where its significant precision loss and recall gain cancel
in $F_1$ (Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}). Neither comparison
shows that the PKB is unnecessary or harmful, but every comparison
favours max-merge or finds no difference, so the paper cannot claim that
it improves held-out $F_1$. The rival ceiling is one mechanism that can
hold back recall under the Noisy-OR rule but not under max-merge; it has
not been shown to cause the ablation difference. The feedback
controller's ablation is a plain null ($-0.032$, 95\% CI
$[-0.090, 0.023]$): neither a benefit nor a cost of about $0.08$ $F_1$ or
more is supported.
% [EVID-045; EVID-041]

\subsection{Why Fine-Tuning Raised Precision but Lowered Recall}
\label{sec:discussion-finetuning}

This is consistent with how the fine-tuning data was built: from the
knowledge base's own high-confidence triples, that is, from facts the
extractor had already found and the PKB had already accepted. Such data
can teach the model to state already-discoverable facts more reliably
and to suppress spurious alternatives, raising precision, but it gives no
exposure to facts the extractor never found, which would be needed to
raise recall. It can also suppress previously recovered true positives
that fall outside the narrower training distribution, lowering recall,
as in every seed of the leakage-free evaluation. That cost was invisible
on the superseded 8-product set, where the base model had only 7 true
positives. Selection also favoured this outcome: validation recall was
flat across configurations, so $F_1$-based selection chose on precision
alone and picked the most conservative adapter. The recall loss explains
the negative per-product result; the positive pooled comparison comes
mainly from false positives removed on products where the base model had
no correct triples. These accounts fit the per-seed detail but do not, on
their own, rule out other explanations.
% [EVID-044]

\subsection{Why Calibration Is Poor}
\label{sec:discussion-calibration}

Two mechanisms are consistent with the poor calibration (ECE $=0.333$;
no correct triples in the $0.6$--$0.7$ and $0.8$--$0.9$ bins). First, the
input confidence is self-reported by the extractor within a
prompt-imposed range, not derived from token probabilities or calibrated
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}), so a poorly calibrated output
is expected from the input alone. Second, saturation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}) applies whether repeated
observations are correct or not, so a repeated incorrect extraction
accumulates confidence exactly like a repeated correct one
(Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}). This fits the two worst bins being
almost entirely single-valued-predicate triples with a median of zero
competing values, the uncontested case in which nothing in
Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} can challenge a confidently repeated wrong
answer. Neither mechanism was isolated experimentally.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{A synthetic primary domain.} Every product-domain result
comes from a deterministic synthetic generator in which the structured
records, the text, and both gold sets are produced by the same function
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}). The domain names each fact
consistently across sources, is fully labelled, and has a fixed
13-predicate schema, which real product data would not. The two public
corpora (Sections~\ref{sec:results-docred}
and~\ref{sec:results-biored}) show how differently the aggregation stage
behaves on real text: on both, aggregation lowered $F_1$ relative to no
aggregation. The product-domain numbers should therefore be read as
results under favourable conditions, not as estimates for deployment.

\paragraph{The provenance gate was tested against a generator-derived
source.} The filter's rise in precision from $93.5\%$ to $100.0\%$ was
measured with a structured source generated from the same attribute
values as the gold triples (Section~\ref{sec:results-provenance}), so
the structured source was never wrong
% [EVID-029]
. DEC-028 partly mitigates this by corrupting the structured source at
5\%, 10\%, and 20\%: the filter's precision advantage decayed from
$+0.991$ to $+0.823$ (95\% CI $[0.688, 0.911]$) but remained
significant at every rate
% [EVID-042]
. That corruption was injected at random by this project, however, and
an independent real-world structured source may be wrong in correlated
ways that random corruption does not model.

\paragraph{The feedback loop is not self-supervised.} The feedback
controller builds its hint from the training-split gold labels: missing
facts and false patterns are computed against the known answers
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-feedback})
% [src/feedback_builder.py; src/experiment_runner.py]
. The closed loop as implemented presupposes labelled training data,
which exists here only because the domain is synthetic. A deployment
without gold labels would need a feedback signal derived from the
knowledge base itself, which was not built or tested.

\paragraph{Ablation power.} With thirty seeds the module ablation
detects effects of $0.070$--$0.086$ absolute $F_1$ with 80\% power
(Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}). Smaller effects of the feedback
controller or the probabilistic knowledge base, in either direction,
are not ruled out
% [EVID-045]
.

\paragraph{The closed-loop test is a single seed with a model-family
confound.} The closed-loop integration test used one seed (43, the best
of three in the standalone evaluation) and compared a Llama-3.1-8B
control with a treatment arm fine-tuned from Mistral-7B, so any
difference between the arms cannot be attributed to fine-tuning alone
(Section~\ref{sec:results-closedloop})
% [EVID-030]
. It was not repeated across seeds or with a Llama-based adapter.

\paragraph{Self-reported confidence from two prompt templates.} The
extraction prompt asks the model for a confidence between $0.80$ and
$0.96$, and the fine-tuning data-generation and evaluation prompt for
one between $0.85$ and $0.99$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}). Neither value is derived
from token probabilities or calibrated, and the two ranges are not on a
common scale, so confidence values from the two paths are not
comparable. The aggregated score is poorly calibrated on real data (ECE
$=0.333$; Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration})
% [EVID-036; EVID-041]
.

\paragraph{Software versions.} No fine-tuning run in this project
pinned the versions of torch, transformers, trl, or bitsandbytes,
including the DEC-027 run that supplies the headline fine-tuning result.
Only the PEFT version can be recovered, from adapter metadata (0.21.0
for every DEC-027 adapter; 0.20.0 for the first DEC-006 adapter). A
record of every installed version is written for runs after DEC-027 only
% [EVID-044]
. The API-hosted extractors are fixed by model identifier alone, and
the provider could change the model served behind an identifier.

\paragraph{The scope of the repeat-counting correction.} R3 corrects
the counting of repeated observations from one source, so it acts only
when the extractor emits the same fact more than once from a single
source. On BioRED it raised the precision of Llama-3.1-8B's admitted
triples by $0.054$ (138 repeats) but had no measurable effect for
DeepSeek-V3.2 (6 repeats). With Llama-3.1-8B its $F_1$ was slightly
lower than R2's, because some discarded repeats were correct
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-biored} and~\ref{sec:discussion-r3})
% [EVID-041; EVID-046; EVID-047]
. Its benefit depends on the extractor, and it does not address the
scarcity of corroboration across sources.

\paragraph{Small admitted counts on DocRED.} On DocRED, R3's precision
gain rests on very few triples: 36 admitted and 9 correct with the exact
key. Its confidence interval is correspondingly wide ($+0.190$, 95\% CI
$[0.084, 0.315]$), and its $F_1$ did not differ from R2's
(Section~\ref{sec:results-docred})
% [EVID-046]
. More generally, both public-corpus runs used sentence-level
extraction without document context, a single fixed admission
threshold ($\tau=0.70$), and, on BioRED, co-mention as a proxy for
annotation-level corroboration.

\paragraph{Unmeasured resource use.} Memory use could not be measured
reliably, because every scale ran in a single process, and GPU
utilisation during fine-tuning was not recorded
(Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability})
% [EVID-023]
. The scalability claims cover runtime only.

\paragraph{An unindexed candidate scan.} The evaluated implementation
scans every previously accepted candidate on each new observation,
which is $O(N)$ per call and $O(N^2)$ cumulative
(Supplementary Section~S3). An indexed variant with
the same aggregation mathematics was benchmarked but not integrated
into the pipeline
% [EVID-036]
. This does not limit the scales evaluated here, where API latency
dominates, but would at larger scale.

\paragraph{The weakest extractor on CaRB.} The pipeline's extractor,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, scored $F_1=0.467$ on CaRB against $0.571$ for
DeepSeek-V3.2, and ranked last of five systems in the 30-sentence pilot
(Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks})
% [EVID-032; EVID-035]
. Every aggregation, filtering, and fine-tuning result in this paper
sits on top of that extractor. The public-corpus results show that
extractor recall, not the aggregation rule, is the binding constraint
on the framework's aggregation stage, so a stronger extractor could
change several of these results.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper evaluated a closed-loop extraction design in which a
language-model extractor populates a probabilistically scored knowledge
base that feeds back into extraction, a design and aggregation rule
attributed throughout to \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}. A formal analysis
of the published conservative Noisy-OR rule shows that any contested slot
of a single-valued predicate scores below $1/(m+1) \le 0.5$ and can never
be admitted at a threshold of $0.5$ or more (RQ1); this ceiling blocked
every contested slot on two real-data snapshots and under a structured
source corrupted at up to 20\%. Counting distinct sources rather than
repeated observations raised the precision of admitted facts on the
product domain, DocRED, and BioRED, but only where the extractor repeats
a fact within a source (RQ2). A controlled evaluation found no end-to-end
benefit from the loop's mechanisms: removing the feedback controller or
the probabilistic knowledge base had no significant effect at thirty
seeds, and leakage-free fine-tuning was negative on its pre-registered
primary metric (RQ3). On DocRED and BioRED, aggregation lowered $F_1$
relative to no aggregation for both extractors tested, because the
extractors recovered fewer than 1.4\% of gold facts from more than one
sentence, leaving a corroboration-gated rule almost nothing to admit
(RQ4).

Each open question could be settled by a specific experiment:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Would aggregation help with a stronger extractor?} Repeat
the DocRED and BioRED runs with document-level and stronger extractors,
and check whether aggregation's $F_1$ relative to no aggregation rises
with the share of gold facts recovered from two or more sources. If it
does not, the constraint lies elsewhere.
\item \textbf{Can the loop work without gold labels?} Replace the
feedback controller's gold-label signal
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-feedback}) with one derived from the
knowledge base, such as disagreement or low aggregated confidence, and
repeat the thirty-seed ablation.
\item \textbf{Do the modules have small effects?} Detecting a $0.04$
$F_1$ effect with 80\% power would need about 110--130 seeds at the
observed variance, under one US dollar of API calls at the measured cost
per run.
\item \textbf{Does fine-tuning help once its confounds are removed?}
Repeat the closed-loop test across seeds with an adapter on the
control's own base model, and the leakage-free evaluation with
provenance-filtered training data.
\item \textbf{Does the provenance gate hold against a real structured
source?} Test it with an independently produced structured source whose
errors may be correlated with the text's.
\item \textbf{Can the scores be calibrated?} Repeat the calibration
measurement with confidences derived from token probabilities, to
separate the input signal's contribution from the aggregation rule's.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Taken together, the evidence supports the formal properties of
the aggregation rule and the narrow, scope-limited value of the
distinct-source correction. It does not support the claim that the
closed loop, as designed and implemented here, improves extraction.
