\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Large language models can extract relational facts from text without
task-specific training, but their output is noisy, and the same fact is
often extracted inconsistently across documents and runs. One response
is to close the loop between extraction and knowledge: store extracted
triples in a knowledge base that scores each fact by how much evidence
supports it, admit only well-supported facts, and feed the knowledge
base back into the extractor through prompting, feedback on missed and
spurious facts, and fine-tuning on the knowledge base's own
high-confidence contents. If it works, extraction improves the knowledge
base and the knowledge base improves extraction.

This design is not new. DySECT \citep{aminnaseri2026dysect} describes
such a closed loop. The loop evaluated in this paper follows it, and its
components are attributed to that work throughout: the architecture
(extraction into a probabilistically scored knowledge base, fed back
into extraction through prompt augmentation and fine-tuning on
knowledge-base-derived data); the aggregation rule, a conservative
Noisy-OR with a mutual-exclusivity penalty for single-valued predicates
(their Equations 1--2); its default shrinkage $\lambda=0.75$; and the
``Encouraging'' and ``Prohibitive'' names for the two feedback
directions, recovering missed facts and suppressing false ones
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}). This paper does not claim
any of these, or that such a system has not been built before.

What such a loop has not had is a controlled evaluation of whether its
mechanisms do what they are designed to do
(Section~\ref{sec:gap}). The published evaluation reports a recall gain
from knowledge-base-guided prompting on one benchmark, without a
module-level ablation, a multi-seed statistical protocol, a calibration
measurement, a held-out test separating the documents that build the
knowledge base from those that measure it, or proofs of the aggregation
rule's properties. This paper provides that evaluation for an
independent implementation of the design, on a synthetic product domain
and two public corpora, DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} and BioRED
\citep{luo2022biored}. It asks
four questions, each matching what was tested:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] What formal properties does the published
aggregation rule have, and can it admit a contested value for a
single-valued predicate, one for which rival values have also been
observed?
\item[\textbf{RQ2}] Does counting distinct sources rather than repeated
observations improve the precision of what the rule admits, and under
what conditions does that correction act?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}] In a controlled evaluation with held-out data,
multiple seeds, and pre-registered tests, do the loop's mechanisms --
the feedback controller, probabilistic aggregation, and fine-tuning on
knowledge-base-derived data -- improve extraction?
\item[\textbf{RQ4}] On real text, does aggregation that requires
corroboration improve on no aggregation, and if not, what limits it?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The paper makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] A formal analysis of the published conservative Noisy-OR
aggregation rule, including the \emph{rival-ceiling} result: for a
single-valued predicate, any contested slot scores below
$1/(m+1) \le 0.5$, where $m \ge 1$ is the number of rival values, and so
cannot be admitted at any threshold at or above $0.5$
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}).
\item[(ii)] An empirical demonstration that the ceiling blocks contested
slots: on two real-data snapshots the published rule admitted none,
against 6 and 40 for a max-merge baseline, and it admitted none under a
structured source corrupted at controlled rates of 5\%, 10\%, and 20\%
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}
and~\ref{sec:results-provenance}).
\item[(iii)] A corrected rule based on distinct-source counting, with
measured precision gains -- on the product domain at the operating
threshold, and on DocRED and BioRED -- and an explicit scope limit: it
acts only when the extractor repeats a fact within a single source, and
had no measurable effect for an extractor that rarely did
(Sections~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical},
\ref{sec:results-docred}, and~\ref{sec:results-biored}).
\item[(iv)] An independent controlled evaluation of the closed-loop
design, finding no end-to-end benefit from its mechanisms. Removing the
feedback controller or the probabilistic knowledge base had no
significant effect at thirty seeds; a leakage-free fine-tuning
evaluation is negative on its pre-registered primary metric; and on
DocRED and BioRED, with two extractors, aggregation lowered $F_1$
relative to no aggregation. The binding constraint on aggregation over
real text is extractor recall, not the key used to match facts: the
extractors recovered only 0.26\% (DocRED) and 0.75--1.35\% (BioRED) of
gold facts from more than one sentence, although 50.2\% of DocRED's
gold facts have two or more evidence sentences and 35.1\% of BioRED's
are co-mentioned in two or more sentences
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-ablation}, \ref{sec:results-finetuning},
\ref{sec:results-docred}, and~\ref{sec:results-biored}).
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related work, including DySECT
in detail, and Section~\ref{sec:gap} states the evaluation gap this
paper addresses. Section~\ref{sec:framework} describes the evaluated
implementation, including what was specified but not built.
Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis} analyses the aggregation rule and
tests corrected rules offline. Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}
describes the datasets, models, and statistical protocol,
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets them.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the limitations, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

The work presented in this paper intersects six established research
paradigms: automated machine learning \citep{hutter2019automl,elsken2019nas,feurer2022autosklearn2,he2021automlsurvey}, information
extraction \citep{banko2007openie,lu2022uie,josifoski2022genie,liu2024openiesurvey}, retrieval-augmented generation \citep{guu2020realm,lewis2020rag}, parameter-efficient
fine-tuning \citep{houlsby2019peft,lester2021prompttuning,hu2021lora,dettmers2023qlora}, feedback-based self-improvement \citep{wang2023selfinstruct,madaan2023selfrefine,gulcehre2023rest,chen2024spin}, and probabilistic knowledge
representation \citep{bach2017psl,ji2021kgsurvey,nguyen2020kgcsurvey}. Each paradigm addresses a partial subset of the
capabilities that SLDE-AFT integrates within a single architecture; the
subsections below review each in turn, and Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}
discusses the one system in the literature that spans the largest share of
these capabilities at once.

\subsection{Automated Machine Learning}

Automated machine learning (AutoML) emerged to reduce human effort in
constructing supervised learning pipelines by automating model selection,
hyperparameter optimisation, and architecture search \citep{hutter2019automl,he2021automlsurvey}.
Bayesian-optimisation-based frameworks such as Auto-Sklearn \citep{feurer2015autosklearn}
and its successor Auto-Sklearn~2.0 \citep{feurer2022autosklearn2} introduced
meta-learning-guided warm starting and portfolio construction to reduce
pipeline search time substantially. Ensemble-driven systems such as
AutoGluon \citep{erickson2020autogluon} extended automation to tabular
prediction tasks through multi-layer stacking, while commercial platforms
such as Amazon SageMaker Autopilot \citep{das2020sagemaker} enabled
transparent, editable pipeline generation at deployment scale. Neural
architecture search methods \citep{elsken2019nas} further widened the
automation boundary to model structure itself. Despite these advances, AutoML
frameworks treat data extraction as a fixed upstream preprocessing step: the
adaptive boundary begins at feature engineering and ends at model deployment,
leaving the connection between heterogeneous source ingestion, knowledge
accumulation, and iterative model adaptation outside the automation scope
addressed by the closed-loop design described in Section~\ref{sec:framework}.

\subsection{Information Extraction and Relation Extraction}

Information extraction research has pursued two broad trajectories:
schema-free open extraction and schema-guided structured extraction. Open
Information Extraction systems \citep{banko2007openie,liu2024openiesurvey}
showed that relational triples could be extracted from large text corpora
without predefining relation types, using lightweight linguistic processing
and redundancy-based confidence estimation. Document-level relation
extraction benchmarks such as DocRED \citep{yao2019docred} established
evaluation standards for reasoning across sentence boundaries, while
supervised relation classification datasets such as TACRED
\citep{zhang2017tacred} became widely adopted benchmarks for sentence-level
relation extraction. Generative extraction architectures reformulated triple
extraction as sequence-to-structure generation: REBEL \citep{cabot2021rebel}
reformulated end-to-end relation extraction as autoregressive triplet
generation, GenIE \citep{josifoski2022genie} extended this to
schema-constrained decoding at large entity-relation scale, and TANL
\citep{paolini2021tanl} treated structured prediction as translation between
augmented natural languages. Universal IE frameworks \citep{lu2022uie,wang2023instructuie}
unified heterogeneous extraction tasks under a single architecture via
structured extraction languages and multi-task instruction tuning. Joint
extraction systems such as DyGIE++ \citep{wadden2019dygiepp} improved the
integration of entity and relation extraction, while open-world attribute
discovery systems such as OA-Mine \citep{zhang2022oamine} enabled discovery
of previously unseen attributes beyond predefined schemas. Zero-shot relation
extraction approaches \citep{levy2017zeroshot} demonstrated that
question-template reformulation can facilitate extraction of previously
unseen relation types without retraining. These approaches improve
extraction accuracy, coverage, schema flexibility, or generalisation
capability, but they generally treat extraction as a terminal process and do
not leverage accumulated extracted knowledge to drive iterative model
adaptation through an automated feedback loop.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) addresses the knowledge limitations of
parametric language models by augmenting generation with dynamically
retrieved external evidence, reducing hallucination and improving factual
grounding. The RAG framework of \citet{lewis2020rag} demonstrated that dense
passage retrieval combined with sequence-to-sequence generation improves
factual accuracy on knowledge-intensive question-answering and reasoning
tasks. REALM \citep{guu2020realm} integrated retrieval directly into language
model pre-training, enabling differentiable retrieval learning while
decoupling parametric knowledge from external evidence sources. SELF-RAG
\citep{asai2023selfrag} introduced adaptive retrieval and self-reflection
tokens that let the model critique its own outputs and selectively retrieve
evidence, improving factual attribution. HippoRAG \citep{gutierrez2024hipporag}
proposed a neurobiologically inspired long-term memory architecture combining
knowledge graph construction with personalised PageRank retrieval, improving
multi-hop reasoning. GraphRAG \citep{edge2025graphrag} extended RAG toward
graph-structured community summarisation, enabling retrieval over
hierarchically organised entity relationships. Despite these advances,
existing RAG frameworks primarily focus on retrieval-enhanced inference and
generally treat retrieved knowledge as a transient resource used only during
generation; accumulated knowledge is not typically used to drive iterative
extraction improvement, automated supervision generation, or continuous
model adaptation.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

The pretrain-then-fine-tune paradigm established by BERT \citep{devlin2019bert}
demonstrated that large-scale bidirectional pretraining followed by
task-specific adaptation can achieve strong downstream NLP performance;
however, adaptation typically relies on task-specific labelled datasets.
Instruction tuning via FLAN \citep{wei2022flan} and RLHF-aligned adaptation
via InstructGPT \citep{ouyang2022instructgpt} showed that natural-language
task descriptions and preference signals can generalise fine-tuned behaviour
to unseen tasks, yet both approaches depend on large-scale human annotation.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods substantially reduced
adaptation costs by updating only a small subset of model parameters.
Representative approaches include adapter layers \citep{houlsby2019peft},
soft prompt tuning \citep{lester2021prompttuning}, LoRA \citep{hu2021lora},
and QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora}, which progressively improved parameter
efficiency while preserving downstream performance. Direct Preference
Optimization \citep{rafailov2023dpo} replaced reward modelling with a
simpler binary classification objective. Although these approaches
significantly reduce adaptation overhead, they generally rely on externally
curated supervision and are initiated through manually defined training
workflows; the automatic generation of supervision signals from accumulated
extracted knowledge, and the continuous triggering of model adaptation from
that signal, remain largely unexplored by this line of work.

\subsection{Feedback-Based and Self-Improving Learning}

A growing line of research investigates how language models can improve
their own outputs through iterative self-generated feedback, without
additional human supervision. SELF-REFINE \citep{madaan2023selfrefine}
showed that a single model acting as both generator and critic can improve
output quality through training-free iterative refinement. Reinforced
Self-Training \citep{gulcehre2023rest} proposed a Grow-Improve loop in which
the model generates candidate outputs, filters them by reward score, and
retrains on high-quality examples offline, reducing the cost of RLHF.
Self-Play Fine-Tuning \citep{chen2024spin} enabled iterative self-improvement
by training a model to distinguish its own outputs from human-quality
references, without additional human data. SELF-INSTRUCT
\citep{wang2023selfinstruct} introduced a self-bootstrapping pipeline for
synthetic instruction generation that reduces annotation dependency.
Toolformer \citep{schick2023toolformer} showed that language models can
acquire external tool-use capabilities through self-supervised data
generation. Continual learning research \citep{wang2024continuallearning}
and Learning without Forgetting \citep{li2017lwf} address stability-plasticity
trade-offs in sequential adaptation settings. These approaches improve
generation quality, self-supervision, or adaptation efficiency, but they
generally operate on transient model outputs and do not maintain a
persistent structured knowledge repository that accumulates extracted facts
and converts them into supervision signals for continuous model adaptation.

\subsection{Probabilistic Knowledge Representation and Explainability}

Principled uncertainty modelling over extracted facts matters for systems
that must decide which knowledge to trust, accumulate, and reuse for
downstream adaptation. Probabilistic Soft Logic \citep{bach2017psl} provided
a scalable framework for soft reasoning over structured relational domains
using hinge-loss Markov random fields, showing that confidence-weighted
inference over structured variables is both theoretically grounded and
practically efficient. Knowledge graph surveys \citep{ji2021kgsurvey,nguyen2020kgcsurvey}
identify confidence-aware extraction, knowledge base population, and
uncertainty management as persistent challenges in scalable knowledge-centric
systems. Explainability research has established principled attribution
methods: LIME \citep{ribeiro2016lime} provides locally faithful
model-agnostic explanations through input perturbation, and LLM
explainability surveys \citep{zhao2023explainability} extend attribution
taxonomies to the prompting and fine-tuning paradigms of large language
models. These works show that confidence modelling and attribution are
tractable and important, but they operate primarily as post-hoc or offline
analysis tools; the integration of provenance traceability and
confidence-aware filtering as \emph{active} components of a continuous
extraction and adaptation pipeline is comparatively underexplored.

\subsection{Closest Prior System: DySECT}
\label{sec:related-work-dysect}

The system closest to SLDE-AFT in the published literature is DySECT, a
Dynamic Self-Evolving Extraction and Curation Toolkit, presented in a paper
titled ``A Dynamic Self-Evolving Extraction System''
\citep{aminnaseri2026dysect}. DySECT incrementally populates a self-expanding
knowledge base with triples extracted by an LLM, aggregates them with a
probabilistic confidence and graph-based reasoning layer built on top of
Theo \citep{mitchell1989theo}, and feeds the enriched knowledge base back
into the extractor through prompt tuning, few-shot example sampling, or
fine-tuning on knowledge-base-derived synthetic data -- forming, in the
authors' own description, a closed loop in which extraction improves
knowledge and knowledge improves extraction. Table~1 of
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} reports that, at the first KB-guided iteration,
recall improves by 5--8 percentage points over the no-feedback baseline
across all four evaluated models (GPT-4.1: 22.80\%$\to$30.62\%; GPT-4.1-mini:
12.72\%$\to$20.22\%; Kimi~K2.5: 32.03\%$\to$37.85\%; LLaMA-3.3~70B:
23.23\%$\to$28.78\%, positive-feedback mode), on DocRED. DySECT also keeps
its knowledge base explicitly inspectable, letting a user review triples,
confidence statistics, and hierarchical structure, and manually validate,
invalidate, or add knowledge.

\paragraph{The confidence aggregation formula is not merely similar --
it is the same formula.} \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s Equations 1--2 define
$C_{\mathrm{agg}}(t) = 1 - \prod_i (1-\lambda c_i)^{f_i}$ with a default
shrinkage $\lambda=0.75$, followed by a mutual-exclusivity penalty
$C(t) = C_{\mathrm{agg}}(t) / (m(t)+1)$. This is notationally and
mathematically identical to SLDE-AFT's Conservative Noisy-OR aggregation
with mutual-exclusivity penalty (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}; the same default shrinkage of
0.75 is independently confirmed in this project's own
\texttt{scripts/dec003\_product\_probkb\_run.py}). This paper does not claim
priority or originality for this formula, and does not claim it is novel or
absent from prior extraction-KB systems: DySECT is a published, citable
counter-example to any such claim. What Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis} contributes instead is
a formal derivation and proofs of this formula's boundedness, monotonicity,
and saturation properties, and the rival-ceiling result
$C(t) < 1/(m(t)+1) \le 0.5$ for any contested single-valued-predicate slot
-- properties stated but not proved in \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s
description of the same formula.

\paragraph{Capabilities SLDE-AFT shares with DySECT.} Four elements of
DySECT's design, as described by \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}, are also
present and verified in SLDE-AFT's implementation (Section~\ref{sec:framework}): a
\emph{closed loop} in which extraction populates a knowledge base and the
knowledge base feeds back into extraction; a \emph{probabilistic confidence}
layer over accumulated triples, rather than treating every extraction as
equally trustworthy; \emph{prompt-augmentation feedback}, in which the
current state of the knowledge base is used to steer the extractor's next
pass; and \emph{synthetic training data generated from high-confidence
knowledge-base triples} to fine-tune the extractor, rather than from an
externally curated dataset.

\paragraph{A second naming match, alongside the formula match above.}
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s Section 4 (``Feedback Modes'') names its two
prompt-augmentation directions \emph{Encouraging (positive)} mode --
high-confidence subconcepts and representative relations injected as
positive examples, encouraging extraction of further instances in similar
concept families -- and \emph{Prohibitive (negative)} mode -- well-covered
or saturated subconcepts marked as already extracted, to discourage
redundant re-extraction. This is the identical naming (``Encouraging
Mode'' / ``Prohibitive Mode'') SLDE-AFT's own architecture design
specifies for its Feedback Controller's two directions -- Encouraging Mode
injecting high-confidence exemplar triples for underrepresented predicates,
Prohibitive Mode flagging false-positive extraction patterns to avoid
% [SLDE_AFT_Revised_Contribution_Document.docx, SLDE.docx -- both use this
% exact "Encouraging Mode"/"Prohibitive Mode" naming for the Feedback
% Controller's two directions, independently of this citation check]
. As with the aggregation formula, this naming is not presented as
SLDE-AFT's own coinage: \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} is a published,
citable source using the identical terms. One implementation-level
difference is worth stating precisely rather than left implied: the
evaluated codebase's feedback builder
(\texttt{src/feedback\_builder.py}) constructs a missing-fact /
false-pattern / undercovered-predicate hint block without using either
literal mode label in code -- the ``Encouraging''/``Prohibitive'' naming
appears in both projects' design-level description of the mechanism, not
necessarily in SLDE-AFT's shipped variable or function names
% [src/feedback_builder.py -- verified directly, no "encouraging" or
% "prohibitive" string anywhere in the module]
.

\paragraph{Capabilities DySECT reports that SLDE-AFT's implementation does
not have.} \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} describe two further capabilities
that were also specified in SLDE-AFT's original architecture design but are
not present in the implementation evaluated in this paper, verified directly
against the current codebase: \emph{hierarchical knowledge abstraction} and
a \emph{human curation interface}.

For hierarchical abstraction, the specific mechanism matches, not just the
general capability: \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s Section 2.2 (``Knowledge
Base Growth'') states that their knowledge integrator ``structures the
KB's concepts into more interpretable hierarchies,'' and that ``for any
node with a large or semantically heterogeneous set of children, we apply
KNN-based clustering over the children's embeddings to group conceptually
similar nodes into coherent clusters,'' with an LLM then proposing a label
for each cluster that becomes a new intermediate node in the hierarchy.
SLDE-AFT's own architecture design specifies the same method: ``Automatic
hierarchical abstraction via KNN clustering and LLM-based subconcept
labeling''
% [SLDE_AFT_Revised_Contribution_Document.docx -- exact quoted phrase,
% verified directly in the document's own text]
-- KNN clustering over embeddings, followed by LLM-generated cluster
labels, in both cases. No such clustering or hierarchy-construction code
exists in SLDE-AFT's evaluated implementation, so this is stated as a
specified-but-unimplemented capability, not a claim that SLDE-AFT
implements a KNN-based hierarchy of its own.

For the human curation interface, SLDE-AFT's design document specified an
analogous human validation interface with character-level source offsets
and a model-version registry with rollback; none of these are implemented
in the evaluated system either.

These are stated here as an honest scope limitation of the current
implementation relative to DySECT, not as a design decision, and are
revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} (Limitations).

\paragraph{Implemented capabilities not described in DySECT.} Two
elements of SLDE-AFT's design have no counterpart described in
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}. First, dual-source structured seeding: SLDE-AFT
ingests structured records (CSV/JSON) through a deterministic schema-based
extraction path at a fixed high confidence, in addition to the LLM-based
unstructured path, before both sources are aggregated by the same
probabilistic mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:framework}); DySECT's knowledge base is populated
from LLM extraction alone. Second, provenance as an automated training-data
gate: rather than a human curation step, SLDE-AFT's provenance layer
automatically withholds a triple from becoming fine-tuning training data
unless it has structured corroboration (Section~\ref{sec:framework}, Section~\ref{sec:results}) -- an
automated mechanism aimed at synthetic-data quality, verified to measurably
change training-data precision on real data (Section~\ref{sec:results}), not a substitute
for DySECT's manual interface but a different kind of gate entirely. The
confidence aggregation formula itself is not unique to SLDE-AFT, as stated
above; the formal analysis of that formula (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}) is what this paper
contributes on top of it.

\paragraph{Empirical comparability.} The two systems' empirical evaluations
use different benchmarks, base models, and metrics: DySECT
\citep{aminnaseri2026dysect} reports a
5--8 percentage point recall lift from KB-guided extraction with no
retraining, and recall improvements on DocRED across GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini,
LLaMA-3.3~70B, and Kimi~K2.5; SLDE-AFT's evaluation
(Sections~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}--\ref{sec:results}) uses CaRB
\citep{bhardwaj2019carb}, DocRED \citep{yao2019docred}, and BioRED
\citep{luo2022biored} with a different set of base models and a
seed-based statistical protocol. No direct numerical comparison between the
two systems is made in this paper as a result; a controlled head-to-head
evaluation on a shared benchmark is identified as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{Synthesis}

Across the paradigms reviewed above, a consistent pattern emerges: each
research tradition addresses one layer of the extraction-knowledge-adaptation
problem in relative isolation. AutoML research automates model selection and
optimisation while leaving knowledge acquisition outside the automation
boundary \citep{hutter2019automl,feurer2022autosklearn2}. Information
extraction systems improve fact discovery but generally treat extracted
knowledge as a terminal output rather than a reusable learning signal
\citep{banko2007openie,lu2022uie,liu2024openiesurvey}. RAG improves grounding
but keeps the retriever static \citep{lewis2020rag,asai2023selfrag}.
Fine-tuning reduces adaptation cost but requires manually curated
supervision and manually triggered training \citep{hu2021lora,dettmers2023qlora}.
Self-improving systems refine outputs but lack a structured, persistent
knowledge substrate \citep{madaan2023selfrefine,gulcehre2023rest}. Provenance
and confidence-modelling systems record attribution but do not typically use
it as an active training-data filter \citep{bach2017psl,ribeiro2016lime}.
DySECT \citep{aminnaseri2026dysect} is the one system in this review that
combines several of these layers into a closed loop, and the preceding
subsection states plainly where SLDE-AFT's design overlaps with it and where
it differs. Section~\ref{sec:gap} formalises the resulting cross-paradigm gap and
positions this paper's evaluation against it.

\section{Cross-Paradigm Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

The six paradigms reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:related-work} each
address one part of the extraction-knowledge-adaptation cycle, and
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} already closes that cycle: a closed loop
in which LLM extraction populates a probabilistically-scored knowledge
base, and the knowledge base feeds back into extraction through prompt
augmentation, few-shot sampling, and optionally fine-tuning
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}). The gap this paper addresses
is therefore not that no system forms such a loop -- one does, and is
cited throughout this paper as prior work, not distinguished from it by
the existence of a loop alone.

The gap is in what has been evaluated about such a loop once it exists.
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s own evaluation reports a recall
improvement from KB-guided prompting, aggregated across four extractor
models on one benchmark, under a single run per configuration
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}). It does not report a
module-level ablation isolating which component of the loop
(aggregation, feedback, fine-tuning) is responsible for a measured
change; a multi-seed statistical protocol distinguishing a real effect
from run-to-run variance; a calibration measurement of the confidence
scores the aggregation rule produces; or a held-out generalization test
separating documents used to build the knowledge base from documents
used to measure it. Symmetrically, this paper does not claim
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s own design contributions -- its
KNN-based hierarchical sub-concept abstraction, its human curation
interface -- as untested weaknesses of that system: SLDE-AFT's own
implementation lacks both (Section~\ref{sec:framework}), so this is not
a comparison this paper is positioned to adjudicate either way.

What SLDE-AFT contributes, then, is not a first closed loop, but a
closed loop of this kind evaluated against the rigor its component
claims require: a formal analysis of the aggregation rule's properties
and failure modes, tested against an offline replay of two independent
real-data snapshots (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}); a
module-level ablation across two scales and up to thirty seeds
(Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}); a multi-seed statistical
protocol with confidence intervals and explicit power limits, stated
rather than implied
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-statistics}, Section~\ref{sec:discussion-ablations});
a real-data calibration measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration}); and a leakage-controlled
held-out evaluation with the leakage rules stated explicitly
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}). Section~\ref{sec:framework}
describes exactly what the evaluated implementation contains, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states plainly where it still falls
short of the design this gap analysis motivates.

\section{Evaluated Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

This section describes only what the evaluated implementation
contains, verified directly against \texttt{src/} and \texttt{scripts/}
this session; a capability specified in the original architecture
design but absent from the code is stated as such, not implied.

\subsection{Dual-Source Extraction}
\label{sec:framework-extraction}

Two extraction paths are implemented for the product domain. The
structured path (\texttt{src/extractors/structured.py}) maps CSV-column
values to the domain's 13 predicates deterministically, at a fixed
confidence of $0.98$, with no LLM call. The unstructured path
(\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_llm.py}) prompts a pinned LLM via
OpenRouter at temperature 0 to extract triples from free text, with a
self-reported confidence the prompt restricts to $0.80$--$0.96$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}). Both paths write to the same
triple schema, which the probabilistic knowledge base then aggregates
jointly. A separate, prompt-parameterized extractor
(\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_openie.py}) is used for CaRB,
DocRED, and BioRED, taking its prompt template as data rather than
hard-coding it, so the same code path serves an open-phrase prompt
(CaRB) or a closed, schema-constrained one (DocRED, BioRED)
% [src/extractors/openrouter_openie.py]
. Two distinct prompt templates are used across the codebase with two
different self-reported confidence ranges: the core extraction prompt
restricts confidence to $0.80$--$0.96$
(\texttt{src/extractors/openrouter\_llm.py}), while the fine-tuning
data-generation and evaluation prompt restricts it to $0.85$--$0.99$
(\texttt{src/prompts.py}) -- stated in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}, restated here as part of what
the extraction modules themselves implement.

There is no dedicated ingestion module (format detection, schema
inference, or chunking): each pipeline reads its own fixed input shape
directly -- Python dictionaries built from CSV/TSV-style records for
the product domain and CaRB
(\texttt{src/datasets/product\_generator.py},
\texttt{src/datasets/carb\_adapter.py}), JSON for DocRED
(\texttt{src/datasets/docred\_adapter.py}), and plain-text PubTator
files for BioRED (\texttt{src/datasets/biored\_adapter.py}). No PDF,
DOCX, or SQL ingestion code exists anywhere in \texttt{src/} or
\texttt{scripts/}, verified by direct search of both directories.

\subsection{Probabilistic Knowledge Base}
\label{sec:framework-pkb}

The Noisy-OR aggregation and conflict-adjustment formulas
(Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict}) are implemented in
\texttt{src/pkb\_math.py}, and applied per accepted candidate by
\texttt{src/pkb\_instrumentation.py}'s \texttt{refresh\_slot\_scores}.
\texttt{src/probkb\_v2\_adapter.py}'s \texttt{CandidateBufferAdapter}
wraps this into a stateful knowledge base: every accepted observation is
kept (no code path in \texttt{src/probkb\_v2\_adapter.py} or
\texttt{src/pkb\_instrumentation.py} deletes, pops, or removes an
accepted candidate, verified directly
% [Results Summary.md, Claim 3 -- verified this session and again here]
), so the knowledge base grows monotonically. A second adapter,
\texttt{src/deterministic\_kb\_adapter.py}'s
\texttt{DeterministicKBAdapter}, implements max-merge (confidence is the
maximum over observations, no conflict handling) behind the identical
interface, so \texttt{src/experiment\_runner.py} can swap between the
two knowledge-base variants for the ablation in
Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation} without branching logic elsewhere.
\texttt{src/pkb\_replay.py} reconstructs a knowledge base's exact
end-of-iteration state by replaying its saved
\texttt{observations\_iteration\_N.csv} files through fresh
\texttt{accept\_candidate()} calls, used for the closed-loop test
(Section~\ref{sec:results-closedloop}) and DEC-026
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}) without any new
extraction.

\subsection{Feedback Controller}
\label{sec:framework-feedback}

\texttt{src/feedback\_builder.py}'s \texttt{build\_feedback\_hint}
constructs the prompt hint used to guide re-extraction: a
missing-facts block (gold keys not yet accepted), a
false-pattern block (accepted keys not in gold), and an
undercovered-predicate block (predicates with fewer than two accepted
observations), assembled from plain-text templates with no named
``mode'' in the code. This function's second argument is
\texttt{gold\_keys}, and every call site that builds a real feedback
hint -- \texttt{scripts/dec003\_product\_probkb\_run.py},
\texttt{src/experiment\_runner.py}, and the corresponding calls in the
other product-domain run scripts -- passes it the \emph{train-split}
gold key set, verified directly this session
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py, lines 184-186 and 218-220;
% src/experiment_runner.py -- build_feedback_hint(kb.as_key_set(),
% train_gold_keys, ALLOWED_PREDICATES) at both the per-iteration and
% held-out-pass call sites]
. The feedback signal is therefore computed against the known gold
answers for the products it is guiding re-extraction on, not against
any uncertainty or disagreement measure internal to the knowledge base
itself -- the closed loop's feedback stage is not self-supervised in
the sense of operating without ground truth; it presupposes gold labels
for the training split are available, which they are only because this
is a synthetic, fully-labeled domain
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}). This is stated here because it
changes what ``closed-loop'' can honestly be claimed to mean for this
implementation, and is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The
design documents' and \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s ``Encouraging''
and ``Prohibitive'' mode names
(Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}) describe the same two
directions this hint encodes (recover missing facts; suppress false
patterns) but do not appear anywhere in \texttt{src/feedback\_builder.py}
-- verified directly, no such string exists in that module -- so this
paper uses the code's own unnamed missing-fact / false-pattern /
coverage-gap description here, not the design documents' mode names,
for what is actually implemented.

\subsection{Synthetic Supervision, Provenance Filtering, and Fine-Tuning}

Synthetic fine-tuning examples are built from the knowledge base's own

(\texttt{scripts/dec006\_regenerate\_synth\_data.py}, using

Six elements of the original architecture design have no corresponding

\section{Formal Analysis of the Aggregation Rule}
\label{sec:formal-analysis}

\subsection{Definition and Attribution}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-definition}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}, the confidence
aggregation rule used throughout this paper is not original to it:
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s Equations 1--2 define the identical
formula, under the identical default shrinkage. This paper cites that
formula rather than claiming it. For a functionally single-valued
predicate slot with candidate object $t$ supported by observations with
self-reported confidences $c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,1]$
% [EVID-041, "confidence is self-reported, not derived from calibration
% or token probabilities" -- verified against src/extractors/openrouter_llm.py]
and shrinkage $\lambda \in (0,1]$ (default $\lambda=0.75$
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py, scripts/dec006_scaleup_probkb_run.py --
% independently confirmed default, matching aminnaseri2026dysect Eq. 1]
), the \emph{conservative Noisy-OR support} is
\begin{equation}
A(t) \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(1 - \lambda c_i\bigr).
\label{eq:noisyor}
\end{equation}
For a slot designated functionally single-valued, with $m(t)$ competing
object values already accepted for the same (subject, predicate) slot,
the admitted confidence is
\begin{equation}
C(t) \;=\; \frac{A(t)}{m(t) + 1}.
\label{eq:conflict}
\end{equation}
For a non-functional predicate (only \texttt{has\_color} in the
product-domain configuration
% [configs/functional_predicates_product_domain.json]
), $C(t) = A(t)$ unconditionally: no mutual-exclusivity penalty is
applied, since multiple simultaneous values are legitimate. A candidate
is admitted into the knowledge base when $C(t) \ge \tau$, with
$\tau=0.88$ the project's standing operating threshold
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py, scripts/dec006_scaleup_probkb_run.py]
throughout the experiments in Sections~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}--\ref{sec:results}.
What this paper contributes on top of Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict}
is (i) formal proofs of properties stated but not proved in
\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s presentation of the same rule, and (ii) an
empirical test, on two independent real-data snapshots, of whether a
corrected rule can outperform it. Both are given below.

\subsection{Formal Properties}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-properties}

\begin{proposition}[Range and boundedness]
For any non-empty finite multiset of confidences $c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,1]$
and $\lambda \in (0,1]$, $A(t) \in [0,1)$ whenever at least one $c_i < 1$,
and $A(t) \to 1$ only in the limit $n\to\infty$ with every $c_i$ bounded
away from $0$. Consequently $C(t) \in [0,1)$ for every functionally
single-valued slot with $m(t) \ge 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each factor $(1-\lambda c_i) \in (0,1]$ since $\lambda c_i \in [0,1]$, so
the product $\prod_i(1-\lambda c_i) \in (0,1]$ and is strictly positive
for finite $n$; hence $A(t) = 1 - \prod_i(1-\lambda c_i) < 1$ strictly for
every finite observation set. $A(t) \ge 0$ follows since each factor is
$\le 1$, so the product is $\le 1$. Dividing by $m(t)+1 \ge 1$ preserves
$C(t) \in [0,1)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Order-invariance]
$A(t)$ depends only on the multiset $\{c_1,\dots,c_n\}$, not on the order
in which observations are accumulated.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Multiplication is commutative, so $\prod_i(1-\lambda c_i)$ is invariant
under any permutation of the index set $i=1,\dots,n$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Monotonicity in evidence]
\label{prop:monotone}
Adding an observation with confidence $c_{n+1} > 0$ to an existing set of
$n$ observations strictly increases $A(t)$; $A(t)$ is non-decreasing in
$n$ and strictly increasing whenever every added observation has
$c_i > 0$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $A_n = 1-\prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i)$. Then
$A_{n+1} - A_n = \prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i) \cdot \lambda c_{n+1} > 0$
whenever $c_{n+1} > 0$, since $\prod_{i=1}^n(1-\lambda c_i) > 0$ by the
boundedness proposition above.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Saturation under repeated observation]
\label{prop:saturation}
If the same confidence $c$ is observed $n$ times, $A_n = 1-(1-\lambda c)^n
\to 1$ as $n \to \infty$, geometrically, at rate $(1-\lambda c)$: the
residual $1 - A_n = (1-\lambda c)^n$ decreases by the constant factor
$(1-\lambda c)$ with every additional identical observation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Immediate from Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} with $c_i=c$ for all $i$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Corroboration requirement]
\label{prop:corroboration}
With $\lambda=0.75$ and $\tau=0.88$, no single observation, regardless of
its confidence, is sufficient for admission: a triple requires at least
two corroborating observations to reach $\tau$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A single observation with the maximum possible confidence $c_1=1$ yields
$A(t) = \lambda = 0.75 < \tau = 0.88$ from Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} with
$n=1$. More generally, admission with $n$ observations each at $c=1$
requires $1-(1-\lambda)^n \ge \tau$, i.e.\ $n \ge \ln(1-\tau)/\ln(1-\lambda)
= \ln(0.12)/\ln(0.25) \approx 1.53$, so $n \ge 2$.
\end{proof}
This property is visible directly in the project's own data: a
functionally single-valued triple supported by exactly one observation
(the common case for a structured-only fact, fixed confidence $0.98$
% [EVID-041, src/extractors/structured.py -- structured confidence
% fixed at 0.98]
) scores $A(t) = \lambda \times 0.98 = 0.735$ under
Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} -- below $\tau=0.88$ regardless of how
trustworthy that single structured source is, exactly as
Proposition~\ref{prop:corroboration} predicts.

\begin{proposition}[Conflict-penalty properties]
\label{prop:conflict}
For fixed $A(t)$, $C(t) = A(t)/(m(t)+1)$ is strictly decreasing in
$m(t)$, and $C(t) = A(t)$ exactly when $m(t)=0$ (no penalty when the slot
is uncontested).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\partial C(t)/\partial m(t) = -A(t)/(m(t)+1)^2 < 0$ for $A(t) > 0$, and
$C(t)|_{m(t)=0} = A(t)/1 = A(t)$ directly from Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Rival ceiling]
\label{prop:ceiling}
For any functionally single-valued predicate slot with $m(t) \ge 1$
competing object values, $C(t) < 1/(m(t)+1) \le 0.5$, independent of how
much observational support $A(t)$ accumulates.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By the boundedness proposition, $A(t) < 1$ for any finite observation
set. Hence $C(t) = A(t)/(m(t)+1) < 1/(m(t)+1)$. For $m(t) \ge 1$,
$1/(m(t)+1) \le 1/2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:ceiling-consequence}
With $\tau = 0.88 > 0.5$, a contested functionally single-valued slot
($m(t) \ge 1$) can never be admitted under Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict},
regardless of how many corroborating observations accumulate for the
leading candidate. Combined with the fact that no candidate is ever
deleted or pruned from the accepted-candidates store anywhere in
\texttt{src/probkb\_v2\_adapter.py} or \texttt{src/pkb\_instrumentation.py}
% [Results Summary.md, Claim 3 -- verified directly, no del/pop/remove
% on the accepted dict]
, a genuinely contested functional slot is not merely slow to resolve
under this rule: it is structurally unresolvable at $\tau=0.88$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proposition}[Independence assumption and repeated observation]
\label{prop:independence}
Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} is a Noisy-OR combination rule, which assumes
each $c_i$ is drawn from a conditionally independent evidence source.
This assumption is violated when the same extractor, invoked at
temperature $0$, is asked to re-read the same source text: the output is
deterministic, so repeated invocations produce byte-identical
observations that are treated by Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} as though they
were independent corroboration, inflating $A(t)$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}'s saturation dynamic regardless of
whether the underlying claim is correct.
\end{proposition}
This is not merely a theoretical caveat: 31 unstructured-only triples in
the DEC-018 200-product snapshot were observed 2--21 times each by
exactly this mechanism, all 31 incorrect against gold
% [EVID-029]
, and the same snapshot's real-data expected calibration error is
$0.3332$
% [EVID-036]
, both consistent with repeated non-independent observation being
mistaken for corroboration. Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}
tests a corrected rule that aggregates over distinct sources instead of
repeat observations, directly targeting this violated assumption.

\subsection{Computational Complexity}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-complexity}

The production candidate-acceptance path is empirically $O(N)$ per call
and $O(N^2)$ cumulative over $N$ accumulated observations
% [EVID-036]
, caused by a linear scan over every previously accepted candidate in
\texttt{src/pkb\_instrumentation.accepted\_slot\_keys} on every new
observation -- measured at $72.7\times$ latency growth for $80\times$
more observations
% [EVID-036]
. An indexed alternative (same aggregation mathematics, keyed by
normalized (subject, predicate) slot rather than scanned linearly)
achieves $O(1)$ per call and $O(N)$ cumulative
% [EVID-036]
; it was implemented illustratively but not wired into the evaluated
system. This is not a limiting factor at the scale evaluated in this
paper ($N$ in the hundreds, Section~\ref{sec:experimental-setup}), where
LLM API latency dominates wall-clock time
% [EVID-036, cross-referenced against DEC-008's linear end-to-end runtime
% finding]
, but is stated here as a forward-looking scalability property of the
current implementation, separate from the aggregation rule's own
mathematical properties above.

\subsection{Empirical Validation: The Ceiling, Tested Corrections, and Their Results}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}

Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling-consequence} is a proof, not a measurement: it
establishes that the ceiling \emph{must} block every contested
single-valued slot, but says nothing about whether contested slots
actually arise on real data, nor whether a corrected rule can do better.
Both questions were tested directly
% [DEC-026, EVID-041]
, offline, by replaying stored observations from two independent
gold-labeled snapshots -- 657 triples from 35 products
% [EVID-013, EVID-014, EVID-036]
and 2241 triples from 140 products
% [EVID-029, EVID-030]
-- through six scoring rules applied to the identical observations (only
the aggregation formula varies), each with its own validation-selected
threshold and a subject-level (product-level) held-out test split (35
and 92 test-split subjects respectively), plus every rule re-evaluated
at the fixed pipeline threshold $\tau=0.88$
% [EVID-041]
.

\paragraph{The ceiling is confirmed empirically, at both thresholds
tested.} The published rule (Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict})
admits exactly zero contested single-valued-predicate slots on either
test split, at either its own F1-selected threshold or the fixed
$\tau=0.88$
% [EVID-041]
. An unconstrained max-merge baseline ($C(t)=\max_i c_i$, no conflict
handling), evaluated identically, admits 6 and 40 contested slots at its
own F1-selected threshold and 20 and 69 at $\tau=0.88$, on the two
datasets respectively
% [EVID-041]
-- confirming that it is the ceiling, and not an absence of contested
slots in the data, that accounts for the published rule's zero.

\paragraph{Tested corrections that are not claimed as contributions.} Two
candidate corrections to Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} were tested and are
reported here as null or negative results, not as improvements:
\begin{itemize}
  \item An evidence-share reformulation, $C(t) = A(t)^2 / \sum_j A(t_j)$
  summed over every candidate $j$ in the same slot, removes the
  algebraic ceiling of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} (it reduces
  correctly to $C(t)=A(t)$ when $m(t)=0$, matching
  Proposition~\ref{prop:conflict}) but is an exact empirical null: it
  produces an admitted set identical to Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} on
  both datasets (bootstrap 95\% CI for the $F_1$ difference exactly
  $[0.0000,0.0000]$ over $10{,}000$ subject resamples)
  % [EVID-041]
  , because no observed contested-slot score in either dataset approaches
  the operating threshold (maximum observed score $0.581$ against
  $\tau=0.73$ on the 657-triple dataset)
  % [EVID-041]
  .
  \item An unsquared variant, $C(t) = A(t)/\sum_j A(t_j)$, was tested
  afterward as an exploratory, post-hoc check and is a negative result:
  it degenerates to $C(t)=1$ for every uncontested slot ($m(t)=0
  \Rightarrow \sum_j A(t_j) = A(t) \Rightarrow C(t)=1$ regardless of the
  actual evidence strength), discarding real signal on $495$ of $495$
  uncontested functional-predicate rows in the 657-triple dataset
  % [EVID-041]
  , and its $F_1$ is significantly worse than the published rule on one
  dataset (95\% CI $[-0.0870,-0.0118]$) and not significantly different
  on the other
  % [EVID-041]
  . This failure is itself a formal, not merely empirical, result: any
  share-based replacement for Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} must satisfy
  $C(t)=A(t)$ when $m(t)=0$ to avoid this degeneracy -- the squared form
  above satisfies this reduction and the unsquared form does not, which
  is why one is empirically inert and the other is empirically harmful,
  independent of which snapshot either is evaluated on
  % [EVID-041]
  .
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The corrected rule: distinct-source aggregation.} The one
tested correction that is claimed as a contribution targets
Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}'s violated-independence source of
error directly, rather than the ceiling: aggregate
Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} over confidences from distinct
$(\text{source\_type}, \text{source\_id})$ pairs (deduplicating repeated
observations from the same source, keeping the maximum confidence per
source) instead of over every repeat observation, leaving
Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}'s conflict handling otherwise unchanged. At
the pipeline's fixed operating threshold $\tau=0.88$ -- the threshold
this paper's other results use, and therefore the relevant comparison --
this correction yields a statistically significant precision gain over
the published rule at \emph{exactly unchanged} recall on both datasets
(bootstrap 95\% CI for the recall difference exactly $[0.0000,0.0000]$
on both):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Dataset & Precision diff.\ (95\% CI) & Recall diff.\ (95\% CI) & $F_1$ diff.\ (95\% CI) \\
\midrule
657 triples / 35 products & $+0.1060\ [0.0321, 0.2267]$ & $0.0000\ [0.0000, 0.0000]$ & $+0.0192\ [0.0057, 0.0395]$ \\
2241 triples / 140 products & $+0.0270\ [0.0076, 0.0519]$ & $0.0000\ [0.0000, 0.0000]$ & $+0.0059\ [0.0016, 0.0115]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
% [EVID-041, tau=0.88 fixed-threshold bootstrap, 10,000 resamples over test-split subjects]
Both the precision and $F_1$ intervals exclude zero on both independent
datasets. The same correction also improves $F_1$ at each dataset's own
F1-selected threshold (+0.0106 $[0.0028,0.0222]$ and +0.0039
$[0.0013,0.0075]$ respectively)
% [EVID-041]
, so the effect is not an artifact of threshold selection. An
unconstrained max-merge baseline also outperforms the published rule at
its own F1-selected threshold, matching the same direction already found
in this project's $N{=}50$ closed-loop ablation
% [EVID-039, AUDIT.md A1]
, but that advantage does not survive at the shared $\tau=0.88$ operating
threshold (bootstrap $F_1$-difference CI includes zero on both datasets,
driven by a significant precision loss that offsets a significant recall
gain)
% [EVID-041]
and is not reported as a corrected rule here, only as further empirical
confirmation of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical},
above).

\subsection{Summary}
\label{sec:formal-analysis-summary}

Three claims follow from this section. First, the aggregation formula
(Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict}) and its default shrinkage
$\lambda=0.75$ are \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}'s, cited here, not
claimed as this paper's contribution (Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}).
Second, this paper's contribution is the formal analysis above --
boundedness, order-invariance, monotonicity, the corroboration
requirement, saturation, the conflict-penalty properties, and the rival
ceiling with its structural-unresolvability corollary -- together with
the empirical demonstration that this ceiling blocks every contested
single-valued slot on two independent real-data snapshots, at both the
F1-selected and the fixed pipeline threshold. Third, the corrected rule
this paper proposes is distinct-source aggregation
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}), reported with its
measured precision and $F_1$ gain over the published rule at
$\tau=0.88$ and the corresponding 95\% confidence intervals above; the
two other candidate corrections tested (evidence-share and its unsquared
variant) are reported as tested and null or negative, not as
contributions of this paper.

\texttt{belongs\_to\_category}, \texttt{has\_price\_usd},
\texttt{has\_screen\_size\_inch}, \texttt{has\_ram\_gb},
\texttt{has\_storage\_gb}, \texttt{has\_battery\_life\_hours},
\texttt{has\_weight\_kg}, \texttt{has\_color}, \texttt{made\_of\_material},
\texttt{target\_market\_region}, \texttt{supports\_fast\_charging},
\texttt{has\_noise\_cancellation})
% [src/datasets/product_generator.py -- ALLOWED_PREDICATES]
. For each product, the generator draws brand, category, color,
material, region, and 8 numeric attributes from fixed pools, producing
(i) a structured record with all 13 facts, (ii) an unstructured text
paragraph that verbalizes a random 7-of-13 subset of those facts as
prose sentences, and (iii) gold triples: \texttt{gold\_structured} (all
13 facts) and \texttt{gold\_unstructured} (the subset actually
verbalized in the text). \textbf{The structured records and the gold
triples used to score every product-domain result come from the same
generator function}
% [src/datasets/product_generator.py -- both gold_structured and the
% structured record are built from the identical drawn attribute values,
% same generate_product_record() call]
-- stated explicitly here since it underlies the provenance-filter
circularity caveat discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} (Limitations).

Two dataset sizes are used, both split 70/10/20 (train/val/test) with
\texttt{random.Random(7)} (a seed distinct from the generation seed)
% [src/leakage_split.py]
: a 50-product superset (35 train / 5 val / 10 test,
\texttt{data/product\_split.csv})
% [EVID-013]
used for the canonical DEC-003 instrumented run and the fine-tuning
evaluation split, and a 200-product superset (140 train / 20 val / 40
test, \texttt{data/product\_split\_200.csv})
% [scripts/dec006_scaleup_probkb_run.py; 140/20/40 counts also verified
% in DEC-024's Decision log entry]
used for the DEC-006 scale-up and DEC-026's second replay dataset.
Leakage rules, applied identically across every experiment that uses
either split
% [src/leakage_split.py -- save_product_split()]
: test-split products are evaluated on unstructured-text extraction
only, never seeded into the knowledge base with their own structured
facts; test-split products are never used in synthetic training data,
fine-tuning data, few-shot examples, feedback-controller exemplars, or
threshold selection; val-split products may be used for threshold
selection and hyperparameter tuning; train-split products may seed the
knowledge base and generate synthetic supervision.

\paragraph{Public benchmarks.} \textbf{CaRB} \citep{bhardwaj2019carb}: 548 of the benchmark's 641
test-split sentences
% [EVID-035]
, selected by exact-string match between \texttt{data/CaRB/data/test.txt}
and its gold annotation file \texttt{test.tsv}; the excluded 93 sentences
are a pre-existing whitespace/quoting mismatch between those two files,
not a deliberate exclusion
% [EVID-035, Limitations]
. \textbf{DocRED}: all 845 documents of the official 998-document
human-annotated dev split (\texttt{thunlp/DocRED} via its Hugging Face
mirror) that contain at least one gold fact supported by two or more
evidence sentences. These documents have 6,861 sentences and 11,344 gold
facts (entity-level head, relation, tail), of which 5,691 (50.2\%) have
two or more evidence sentences in the gold annotations. That is genuine
multi-sentence corroboration in the text for the PKB to aggregate over,
exercising the framework's aggregation mechanism, not only its
extractor. The extractor is run on every sentence
% [Decision log.md, DEC-031; EVID-046]
. An earlier 15-document pilot from the same pool sent only the gold
evidence sentences to the extractor, an oracle setting that tells the
extractor which sentences contain a relation. It is superseded by the
845-document run and is reported only as historical context
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020, DEC-031]
. \textbf{BioRED} \citep{luo2022biored}: all 500 abstracts of the official Train (400) and
Dev (100) splits (PubTator format, obtained from NCBI's public FTP
distribution); the Test split is not used. They contain 5,462 sentences
(the title plus the abstract, split by a fixed rule) and 4,906 gold
facts, each an unordered pair of annotated concepts with one of eight
relation types. BioRED does not annotate evidence sentences. As a proxy,
35.1\% of gold facts have both concepts mentioned together in two or
more sentences, and 89.7\% in at least one
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032; EVID-047]
. An earlier 15-abstract pilot sent each whole abstract to the
extractor in a single call, so every document had one source and no
aggregation was possible. It tested the extractor only, never the
framework, and is superseded
% [EVID-024; EVID-047]
.

\subsection{Hyperparameters and Prompts}
\label{sec:setup-hyperparameters}

The aggregation rule (Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict},
Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}) is applied with different
hyperparameters in different settings, not a single fixed pair
throughout the paper: the \textbf{product domain} uses shrinkage
$\lambda=0.75$ and admission threshold $\tau=0.88$
% [scripts/dec003_product_probkb_run.py, scripts/dec006_scaleup_probkb_run.py]
, the same default \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} report; \textbf{DocRED}
uses $\lambda=0.5$ and $\tau=0.70$, recalibrated down from the product
domain's defaults because $\tau=0.88$ would require four or more
corroborating observations under $\lambda=0.5$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:corroboration}'s argument applied with these
values), which DocRED's evidence structure rarely supplies, whereas
$\tau=0.70$ is reachable with two
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020]
; no functional-predicate conflict-adjustment policy is applied to
DocRED (\texttt{configs/docred\_functional\_predicates.json} is left
empty), so only Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor}'s Noisy-OR support is used
there, not Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}'s conflict penalty
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020]
; \textbf{BioRED} uses the DocRED values ($\lambda=0.5$, $\tau=0.70$)
and likewise no conflict penalty
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032]
. The
product-domain closed loop runs 4 extraction iterations for train-split
products (structured facts seed the knowledge base at iteration 1 only;
the LLM re-extracts every iteration, guided from iteration 2 onward by a
feedback hint built from the accumulated knowledge base); held-out
val/test-split products receive exactly one unstructured-only extraction
pass, guided only by the trained knowledge base's locked context, never
by their own structured facts
% [EVID-013]
. DocRED and BioRED do not use this iterative loop: each document is
processed once, one extraction call per sentence, and the same fact
extracted from several sentences (not from several extraction
iterations) supplies the Noisy-OR aggregation's repeated observations.

Two distinct prompt templates are used, with two different
self-reported confidence ranges -- in both cases the confidence value is
produced by the model itself in response to a prompt instruction, not
derived from token probabilities, log-likelihoods, or any external
calibration procedure
% [EVID-041; verified directly against src/extractors/openrouter_llm.py]
: the core pipeline's extraction prompt
(\texttt{src/extractors/} \texttt{openrouter\_llm.py}) instructs a
confidence between $0.80$ and $0.96$
% [src/extractors/openrouter_llm.py, line 35]
, while the fine-tuning data-generation and evaluation prompt
(\texttt{src/prompts.py}, used identically for building synthetic
training examples and for evaluating the fine-tuned model, so the two
tasks match) instructs a confidence between $0.85$ and $0.99$
% [src/prompts.py, line 28]
-- a deliberately different range from the pipeline prompt, not a typo,
and stated here so neither range is mistaken for the other when
confidence values are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}. CaRB,
DocRED, and BioRED each use their own dedicated open-information-
extraction prompt (\texttt{prompts/openie\_carb\_v1.txt}
% [EVID-021]
, \texttt{prompts/openie\_docred\_v1.txt}
% [Decision log.md, DEC-020]
, \texttt{prompts/openie\_biored\_sent\_v1.txt}, a per-sentence version
of the earlier pilot's \texttt{prompts/openie\_biored\_v1.txt} that lists
all eight BioRED relation types where the pilot's listed six
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032]
respectively), each schema-appropriate to its domain (CaRB's is
open-phrase, DocRED's and BioRED's enumerate their respective closed
relation sets).

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:setup-metrics}

Unless stated otherwise, triples are scored by normalized exact-match
set comparison (\texttt{src/evaluator.py}: subject/predicate/object
lower-cased and whitespace-normalized, then intersected as sets for
true/false positives and false negatives)
% [src/evaluator.py]
, used throughout the product domain and as this project's own internal
CaRB metric. \textbf{CaRB} results are reported from two scorers, not
one: this internal exact-match evaluator, which is known to undercount
matches that the official scorer accepts
% [EVID-031]
, and the official \texttt{CaRB} scorer (lenient span-overlap matching,
reporting AUC and an optimal precision/recall/F1 operating point) -- the
official scorer is the number this paper cites for CaRB
% [EVID-031, EVID-035]
; its AUC should be read as a single-point approximation, since no
per-triple confidence score is produced beyond one operating point, not
as a full precision-recall curve
% [EVID-035, Limitations]
. \textbf{BioRED} is scored in the same way as DocRED (described next), against gold
concepts rather than strings. A predicted triple matches a gold fact
when its relation type matches and its two arguments, in either order,
equal after normalisation any annotated mention of the two concepts.
Exact matching against each concept's first mention and a relaxed
containment match (either normalised string contains the other), the
earlier pilot's two rules, are reported as secondary measures
% [Decision log.md, DEC-032; EVID-047]
. \textbf{DocRED} is scored per document against gold entities rather
than strings. A predicted triple matches a gold fact when its relation
name matches and its subject and object, after the string normalisation
defined in Section~\ref{sec:results-docred}, equal the normalised form of
any gold mention of the head and tail entities. Predictions are
deduplicated per gold fact, and the same evaluator is used for every
aggregation arm. Exact matching against each entity's first mention, as
in the earlier pilot, is reported as a secondary measure
% [Decision log.md, DEC-031; EVID-046]
.

\paragraph{Recall denominator.} Because this paper reports recall
figures with more than one possible denominator, the definition is
stated once here, at its first appearance
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}'s DEC-026 results are the
first in this paper to require it), so no later recall number needs to
be inferred rather than looked up. DEC-026's recall is $TP/(TP+FN)$
against \texttt{gold\_unstructured} facts for train-split products,
imported directly from the same \texttt{normalize\_gold()} logic the
original DEC-003/DEC-006 run scripts use for their own gold sets -- the
identical set those scripts' own \texttt{gold\_label} snapshot column is
built from (verified: zero mismatches against that column across both
snapshots)
% [EVID-041]
. This denominator is \emph{directly comparable} to EVID-014's whole-KB
admission recall on the same 657-triple snapshot, at the same $\tau=0.88$
(both use the identical gold set and the identical snapshot)
% [EVID-041]
, but is \emph{not} comparable to two other recall figures elsewhere in
this paper's results: EVID-013's held-out validation/test recall is
computed on different, non-train-split products, from a single raw
unstructured-extraction pass with no confidence-threshold admission step
at all -- an extraction-recall quantity, not an admission-recall one
% [EVID-013; EVID-041]
; and DEC-019's closed-loop recall (Section~\ref{sec:results}) is
computed against \texttt{gold\_structured} -- every structured-column
fact per product, a materially larger and stricter denominator than
\texttt{gold\_unstructured} -- verified directly against
\texttt{scripts/} \texttt{dec019\_closedloop\_compare.py}'s own gold
construction, not assumed
% [EVID-041]
. Wherever a recall number appears in Section~\ref{sec:results}, its
specific denominator is named next to it rather than left implicit.

\subsection{Statistical Protocol}
\label{sec:setup-statistics}

\paragraph{Multi-seed confirmatory comparisons.} The fine-tuning
results and the $N=20$ ablation use five seeds, $42$--$46$; the $N=50$
ablation uses thirty, $42$--$71$
% [EVID-020, EVID-039, EVID-040, EVID-044, EVID-045]
, reporting the mean and \emph{sample} standard deviation ($\text{ddof}=1$)
% [scripts/dec006_5seed_stats.py]
across seeds, a one-sample $t$-test against a fixed base-model score, and
a paired $t$-test plus Wilcoxon signed-rank test between two
configurations evaluated on the same seeds
% [scripts/dec006_5seed_stats.py; EVID-040]
. At $n=5$, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test has a floor of $p=0.0625$: this
is the smallest $p$-value the test can report at this sample size,
reached when every one of the 5 paired differences has the same sign
(the maximum-strength result available, not an artifact of a weak
effect)
% [EVID-040]
; this floor is reached in DEC-027's secondary, descriptive
whole-set comparison (EVID-044, 5/5 seeds positive) and in the
superseded, tuned-on-test EVID-040 comparison, and in both cases it
reflects the $n=5$ limit rather than a weaker effect than the
accompanying $t$-tests.

\paragraph{Tuning-leakage caveat (AUDIT.md A2) and its resolution
(DEC-027).} The epoch/LoRA hyperparameter grid used to select the
earlier epochs$=5$ configuration (Stages~1--2) and the five-seed
confirmatory run that reported $p=0.0028$ against it (Stage~3) both
evaluated on the identical 8-product test split -- 8, not the full 10
test-split products from Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}'s 50-product
superset, because an automated leakage guard excludes any test product
whose name is also found embedded in the fine-tuning training data,
and excludes 2 of the 10 here
% [scripts/dec006_evaluate_adapter.py; EVID-037]
. There was no independent validation split separating which
configuration was selected from how well it was confirmed. DEC-024
pre-registered the fix (select hyperparameters on the 200-product
superset's 20-product validation split, confirm once on its untouched
40-product test split); it was executed as DEC-027, with an additional
pre-registered leakage-guard unit test
(\texttt{tests/test\_dec027\_leakage\_guard.py}) that hard-aborts
training before any GPU time is spent if a held-out product is found
in the training data
% [Decision log.md, DEC-027; EVID-044]
. The leakage-free replication's primary, pre-registered test is
negative (95\% CI excludes zero on the negative side) and supersedes the earlier
$p=0.0028$ result as this paper's headline fine-tuning claim
(Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning});
Section~\ref{sec:results}'s citation of the earlier $p=0.0028$ result
is retained only as historical context and carries this supersession
note every time it is stated, not as an aside.

\paragraph{Bootstrap confidence intervals.} DEC-026's aggregation-rule
comparison (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}) uses a
subject-level (product-level) cluster bootstrap, $10{,}000$ resamples
with replacement drawn over test-split subjects, with true/false
positive/negative counts summed per resample from each subject's own
counts (so a subject drawn more than once contributes its counts that
many times) before precision, recall, and $F_1$ are recomputed from the
resampled totals; the reported 95\% CI is the $[2.5,97.5]$ percentile
interval of the resulting distribution
% [EVID-041; scripts/dec026_aggregation_rules_replay.py]
. DEC-027's fine-tuning comparison uses a paired bootstrap over the 40
test products instead (mean per-product $F_1$ difference, fine-tuned
minus base, averaged across the five confirmatory seeds; $10{,}000$
resamples, same $[2.5,97.5]$ percentile-interval convention), following
this project's own established convention for separating seed-level
variance from test-set-level uncertainty
% [Decision log.md, DEC-027; EVID-044]
; Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning} reports this as the primary
fine-tuning result, which is negative (95\% CI excludes zero on the
negative side).

\paragraph{Null-result convention.} Applied consistently across DEC-005,
DEC-023, DEC-024's design, and DEC-026: $p \ge 0.05$ (or, for a
bootstrap comparison, a 95\% CI that includes zero) is reported as a
null result exactly as found, with no re-running, additional seeds, or
post-hoc adjustment undertaken to chase significance
% [README.md, ground rule 5]
.

\subsection{Note on DEC-026}
\label{sec:setup-dec026-note}

DEC-026 (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}) is a
\textbf{replay} over knowledge-base snapshots already saved by the
DEC-003 and DEC-006 product-domain runs described in
Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}: it re-scores already-collected
observations under six aggregation formulas, computing every quantity
reported for it from those saved CSV files. No API calls and no GPU
time were spent obtaining any DEC-026 result; its cost is the
wall-clock time of a local Python/pandas script
% [EVID-041; Decision log.md, DEC-026]
.

exact key (lower-cased, stripped). The second is a deployable string
normalisation: Unicode NFKC, case folding, removal of possessives,
periods and apostrophes, other punctuation replaced by spaces,
whitespace collapsed, and one leading article dropped. The third keys
each string to the gold entity whose mention it matches. This gold-alias
key uses the gold annotations and is an oracle upper bound, not a
deployable method. Normalisation left recall unchanged: it admitted 7
more items than the exact key, all false positives, and closed about 0\%
of the $F_1$ gap to no aggregation. The oracle key raised $F_1$ by
$0.0031$ (95\% CI $[0.0017, 0.0047]$), closing 7.3\% of that gap, and
remained $0.039$ below no aggregation (95\% CI $[-0.044, -0.034]$). Of
the 11,344 gold facts, only 29 (0.26\%) were recovered by the extractions

extracted triples used one of DocRED's relation names

discarding 154 and 194 within-sentence duplicate observations
respectively. The counts are small (36 triples admitted, 9 correct, with

analysis

\subsection{Probabilistic Knowledge Base: Real-Data Validation and Calibration}
\label{sec:results-calibration}

On a gold-labelled snapshot of 657 product-domain triples,
conflict-adjusted scoring increased $F_1$ by $0.043$ relative to
unadjusted Noisy-OR support (single run). This compares two forms of
the same rule; it is not a comparison with a no-aggregation or
max-merge baseline. On the 117 triples with at least one competing
value, $F_1$ decreased from $0.185$ to $0.167$, and 108 of these 117
conflicts were between two incorrect values, whereas one was between a
correct and an incorrect value
% [EVID-014]
.

The per-observation confidence entering the aggregation is
self-reported by the LLM, which the extraction prompt restricts to
$0.80$--$0.96$
% [src/extractors/openrouter_llm.py, line 35]
. For single-valued predicates (12 of 13), a slot with at least one
competing value has a final score below $1/(m+1) \le 0.5$
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis}, Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}), so
it cannot reach $\tau=0.88$ while the rival persists
(Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}). The effect of this ceiling on recall was
not measured directly here (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}
measures it offline via DEC-026, Section~\ref{sec:results-dec026}).

\subsection{Module-Level Ablation Study}
\label{sec:results-ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-n50} compares the full pipeline with variants
lacking the feedback controller or using max-merge in place of the
Noisy-OR PKB, at $N=50$ products (10 held-out test products) over
thirty seeds (42--71): the five seeds of the original run plus 25
added to raise statistical power, with the design otherwise unchanged
% [EVID-039; EVID-045; Decision log.md, DEC-029]
. Neither variant differed significantly from the full pipeline in
held-out test $F_1$.

Removing the feedback controller lowered held-out $F_1$ by $0.032$
(95\% CI $[-0.090, 0.023]$, $p=0.28$), a null result. Removing the
Noisy-OR PKB raised it by $0.034$ (95\% CI $[-0.010, 0.082]$,
$p=0.16$). The point estimate therefore favours removing the PKB, and
the interval only just includes zero. This agrees with the
aggregation-rule replay, in which max-merge matched or exceeded the
Noisy-OR rule (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}), and with
the rival ceiling (Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}), which blocks
contested single-valued slots that max-merge admits. On this evidence
the paper cannot claim that the probabilistic knowledge base improves
held-out $F_1$
% [EVID-045; EVID-041]
.

The PKB figure uses each run's first pass. The run protocol, inherited
from the original ablation, re-runs any configuration--seed pair in
which at least 20\% of extraction calls fail (unparsable model output;
every API call returned HTTP 200), retrying only the failed calls. Four
of the 75 new runs triggered it. Three of them were runs without the
PKB, and no full-pipeline run triggered it. In all three the retry
raised held-out $F_1$, so the rule was applied unevenly in the
direction of the observed effect. With retries included, the PKB
difference is $+0.048$ (95\% CI $[-0.003, 0.101]$, $p=0.087$), still
not significant. The feedback-controller result is identical under
both versions
% [EVID-045]
.

With thirty seeds, a paired $t$-test has 80\% power at $\alpha=0.05$
for a standardized effect of $d=0.53$. Given the observed variance,
this is an absolute difference of $0.070$--$0.086$ $F_1$ (range over
both comparisons, using either the ablated configuration's SD or the
SD of the paired differences), compared with about $0.29$ at five
seeds. The null therefore rules out an effect of either component of
roughly $0.08$ $F_1$ or larger, not only a large one. It does not rule
out smaller effects
% [EVID-045; AUDIT.md]
. The five-seed results at $N=20$ and $N=50$
(Table~\ref{tab:s3-ablation-full}) point the same way: all $p \ge 0.31$,
and the max-merge variant scored higher than the full pipeline at both
scales ($+0.147$; $+0.033$)
% [EVID-020; EVID-039]
.

\subsection{Automated Fine-Tuning Evaluation}
\label{sec:results-finetuning}

An earlier fine-tuning evaluation (reported below as historical
context) selected its epoch and LoRA hyperparameters on the same
8-product test set later used to report significance against the base
model -- a tuning-leakage confound (AUDIT.md A2). DEC-027 repeats the
evaluation with hyperparameters selected on an independent 20-product
validation split and confirmed, once, on an untouched 40-product test
split of the 200-product dataset (280 gold triples), using a
pre-registered paired bootstrap over test products as the primary
statistical test; this is the paper's headline fine-tuning result
% [Decision log.md, DEC-027; EVID-044]
.

On the validation split, a seed-42 epoch search selected 8 epochs
(rank 16, $\alpha=32$, learning rate $2\times10^{-4}$; $F_1=0.744$,
ahead of 5 epochs at $F_1=0.722$ and 3 epochs at $F_1=0.646$), and a
follow-up LoRA search at 8 epochs found no configuration exceeding
this default (rank 32 and learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$ both reached
$F_1=0.734$, $0.011$ lower, so the pre-registered $0.01$ tie rule was
not triggered)
% [EVID-044]
. Validation recall was flat across every configuration with at least
5 epochs ($R=0.593$--$0.600$, i.e.\ 83--84 of 140 gold triples), so
selection was decided by precision alone: the top three configurations
were within $0.011$ $F_1$ on 20 products, separated only by the number
of false positives (0 vs.\ 5). Selecting on $F_1$ therefore chose the
most conservative adapter, the one that made the fewest validation
predictions (83), and on 20 products the choice among the top
configurations is close to arbitrary
% [EVID-044]
. On the untouched test split, a fresh base-model evaluation scored
$P=0.568$, $R=0.464$, $F_1=0.511$; the winning configuration, trained
at five seeds, scored $F_1=0.530$--$0.573$ (Table~\ref{tab:finetune-dec027}),
raising precision in every seed ($P=0.704$--$0.894$) but
\emph{lowering} recall in every seed ($R=0.414$--$0.425$ vs.\ base
$0.464$)
% [EVID-044]
.

The pre-registered primary test -- a paired bootstrap of the mean
per-product $F_1$ difference (fine-tuned minus base, averaged across
the five seeds) over the 40 test products, 10,000 resamples -- gives a
point estimate of $-0.021$, 95\% CI $[-0.042, -0.003]$: the interval
excludes zero on the negative side, and the pre-registered positive
criterion (CI excludes zero \emph{and} point estimate $\geq 0.03$) is
not met. This is a negative result on this paper's primary,
pre-registered fine-tuning test, and per the DEC-027 pre-registration
it supersedes the earlier 8-product result below as the headline
fine-tuning claim
% [EVID-044]
.

A secondary, descriptive comparison of whole-test-set $F_1$ against the
same base score is, by contrast, positive ($t(4)=5.37$, $p=0.006$;
Wilcoxon $p=0.063$, the $n=5$ floor). Every test product has exactly
7 gold triples, so the two tests do not disagree because of gold
weighting. They disagree because of where the change falls. The base
model scores $F_1=0$ on 21 of the 40 products while still making 134
false positives there. The fine-tuned model cuts these to about 33,
often by predicting nothing for the product. Pooled precision credits
each removed false positive, but those products stay at $F_1=0$, so the
per-product mean does not move. On the 19 products the base model gets
partly right, true positives fall from 130 to 117.6 (seed mean) while
false positives there were already near zero (3 to 0.6), so nothing
offsets the recall loss. Twenty-eight products are unchanged, nine are
worse (about 1.4 true positives lost each), and three are better. The
pre-registration specified the per-product bootstrap as primary, so the
negative result is reported as this paper's result, not the more
favorable pooled comparison
% [EVID-044]
.

The pooled figures above use the evaluator's convention of scoring all
products' predictions as one global set, in which an identical
incorrect triple predicted for several products counts as a single
false positive. Scoring each product separately and summing counts it
once per product. True positives are identical under both conventions
(130 for the base model), but the base model repeats 38 incorrect
triples across products (mostly ones with a generic subject such as
``smartwatch device''), so its false positives are 99 under the global
convention and 137 under the per-product convention. Per-product
counting gives base $F_1=0.475$ against a seed mean of $0.546$
($+0.071$, $t(4)=7.01$, $p=0.002$); the global convention reported in
Table~\ref{tab:finetune-dec027} gives $0.511$ against $0.553$
($+0.042$, $p=0.006$). The primary bootstrap already scores products
separately and is unaffected
% [EVID-044]
.

single deterministic score; comparisons with it are one-sample $t$-tests.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This section interprets the results reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}; no new numbers are introduced here beyond
those already given there.

\subsection{Why Aggregation Fails on DocRED and BioRED, and the Divergence from DySECT}
\label{sec:discussion-docred}

On 845 DocRED documents, PKB aggregation lowered $F_1$ relative to no
aggregation under every matching key, including an oracle key built from
gold entity mentions (Section~\ref{sec:results-docred}). An earlier
account in this project attributed the same result, on a 15-document
pilot, to exact-string matching: the same fact phrased differently in
different sentences would never share a key. The larger run tested that
account directly and does not support it. A deployable normalised key
closed none of the gap, and the oracle key closed 7.3\%. Pooling was not
the bottleneck: the oracle key already pooled 28 of the 29 gold facts
that the extractions recovered from more than one sentence
% [EVID-046]
.

The failure comes from the scarcity of cross-sentence corroboration in
what the extractor produces. Half of DocRED's gold facts have two or
more supporting sentences in the text, but the sentence-level extractor
recovered only 645 of 11,344 facts at all, and only 29 from two or more
sentences. An aggregation rule that admits a fact only when it is
corroborated cannot admit facts that are never corroborated, whatever
the key (Proposition~\ref{prop:corroboration}). On DocRED the binding
constraint is therefore extractor recall, compounded across sentences,
not the aggregation rule's matching. In the product domain the same fact
is extracted repeatedly across iterations and sources, so the constraint
does not bind there. The framework's aggregation stage therefore adds
value only when the extractor recovers the same fact from more than one
source often enough. This is a boundary condition of the design, and a
stronger or document-level extractor would move it
% [EVID-046]
.

The same pattern holds on BioRED, a second domain with different text,
a different relation schema, and an external baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:results-biored}). A third of BioRED's gold facts are
co-mentioned in two or more sentences, but the extractors recovered only
0.75\% (Llama-3.1-8B) and 1.35\% (DeepSeek-V3.2) of facts from more than
one sentence, and aggregation lowered $F_1$ for both. The stronger
extractor recovered significantly more corroborated facts but still far
too few. It also lost more $F_1$ to aggregation, because both extractors'
aggregated output ended near the same low $F_1$ while DeepSeek-V3.2's
unaggregated output was more precise. A larger aggregation loss for the
stronger extractor therefore reflects its better baseline, not a second
cause of failure
% [EVID-047]
.

\citet{aminnaseri2026dysect} report a consistent recall
\emph{improvement} from KB-guided extraction on DocRED across all four
evaluated models (Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}). The two results
are not a controlled comparison of the same mechanism, and two
differences in design may account for the opposite direction; neither
was tested here:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Different extractors.} \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}
evaluate GPT-4.1, GPT-4.1-mini, LLaMA-3.3~70B, and Kimi~K2.5; SLDE-AFT
uses only \texttt{meta-llama/llama-3.1-8b-instruct}
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-models}), a smaller model in none of their four.
Given the finding above, extractor recall is likely to matter most.
\item \textbf{A different underlying question.} DySECT's reported recall
is extraction recall under KB-guided \emph{prompting} (more triples
found when the prompt is augmented with hierarchical abstractions), not
recall after a confidence-threshold \emph{admission} step. SLDE-AFT
measures the latter, at $\lambda=0.5$, $\tau=0.70$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}), so the two papers' recall
numbers may not measure the same quantity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Rival Ceiling Generalizes Beyond This Data}
\label{sec:discussion-ceiling}

DEC-026 found zero contested single-valued-predicate slots admitted
under R2 (the published rule) on either dataset, at either the
F1-selected or the fixed $\tau=0.88$ threshold, against 6 and 40 such
admissions under the unconstrained R1 baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}). This is not an empirical
regularity specific to the two product-domain snapshots tested: it
follows directly from Equation~\ref{eq:conflict}'s algebraic form
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}, Corollary~\ref{cor:ceiling-consequence}),
which gives $C(t) < 1/(m(t)+1) \le 0.5$ for any slot with $m(t)\ge 1$
competing values, independent of domain, extractor, or how much
evidence accumulates. Consequently, the ceiling applies to \emph{any}
deployment of Equations~\ref{eq:noisyor}--\ref{eq:conflict} with
$\tau \ge 0.5$, not only to the two snapshots DEC-026 replayed -- those
snapshots confirm empirically that the ceiling has a real, non-vacuous
effect (contested slots do arise and R1 does admit some of them), not
that the ceiling itself is data-dependent.

\subsection{Why the Repeat-Counting Correction Succeeds Where Evidence-Share Does Not}
\label{sec:discussion-r3}

R3 (distinct-source aggregation) is the one tested correction with a
statistically robust improvement over R2 at the fixed operating
threshold (Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}); R4 (evidence-share)
is an exact null and R6 (its unsquared variant) is negative
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}). One mechanistic account
consistent with all three results, and with Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}'s
independence-assumption argument, is that the defect these three rules
target is not the same defect: Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} treats every
repeated observation as independent evidence, but repeated invocations
of one extractor at temperature 0 re-reading the same source text are
not independent -- they are deterministic repeats
(Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}). R3 corrects this at the
\emph{input} to the aggregation, by deduplicating repeated observations
from the same source before Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} is applied. R4
and R6 instead rescale the \emph{output} of the same, already
over-counted $A(t)$ -- they change how a contested slot's confidence is
divided, not how many times a repeated observation is counted in the
first place, so neither corrects the mechanism the independence
violation actually damages. R6's specific failure mode illustrates why
rescaling the output is not merely insufficient but can actively hurt:
because $A(t)/A(t)=1$ for any uncontested slot regardless of the
evidence behind it, an unsquared share collapses to a constant on
exactly the slots where the input actually carries information
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}) -- a structural property
of that formula, not an artifact of R6 being tested on this particular
data (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}'s conclusion). This
account is offered as the explanation consistent with the measured
pattern across R3, R4, and R6; it was not independently tested against
alternative accounts of the same three results. The same defect appears
outside the product domain. On DocRED, the extractor emitted the same
triple more than once from a single sentence 154 times (exact key), and
R2 counted these repeats as corroboration. R3 discarded them and raised
the precision of admitted triples from $0.060$ to $0.250$, although on
too few triples to change $F_1$
(Section~\ref{sec:results-docred})
% [EVID-046]
.

This also defines R3's scope. Because it corrects repeat-counting, it
acts only when the extractor emits the same triple more than once from
a single source. On BioRED, Llama-3.1-8B did so 138 times and R3 raised
its precision by $0.054$. DeepSeek-V3.2 did so 6 times and R3 had no
measurable effect (Section~\ref{sec:results-biored}). Where the discarded
repeats include correct facts, R3 can also cost recall: on BioRED its
$F_1$ with Llama-3.1-8B was slightly lower than R2's. R3 is therefore a
correction for extractors that repeat themselves within a source, not a
general improvement to aggregation
% [EVID-047]
.

\subsection{What the Ablations Do and Do Not Show}
\label{sec:discussion-ablations}

Two distinct results both concern whether the Noisy-Or PKB
mechanism helps the pipeline, and neither should be read as stronger
than it is. First, the module-level ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}), extended to thirty seeds, found
no significant difference between the full pipeline and a max-merge
variant ($+0.034$ for max-merge, 95\% CI $[-0.010, 0.082]$). At thirty
seeds the design detects effects of about $0.07$--$0.09$ absolute
$F_1$ with 80\% power, against about $0.29$ at five seeds
% [EVID-045; AUDIT.md]
. So an effect of that size in the PKB's favour is ruled out, but a
smaller one is not, and the point estimate points the other way.
Second, and separately, DEC-026's own
max-merge baseline (R1) numerically exceeded the published rule (R2) in
$F_1$ when each rule was evaluated at its own independently-selected
threshold (Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-empirical}), matching the
module-level ablation's direction -- but this advantage did not survive
when both rules were compared at the single, shared $\tau=0.88$
operating threshold instead (Table~\ref{tab:dec026-bootstrap-tau088}):
R1's precision loss and recall gain at that threshold are each
individually significant but cancel in $F_1$, whose confidence interval
includes zero on both datasets. Taken together, these results do
not show that the Noisy-Or PKB mechanism is unnecessary or harmful:
neither comparison reaches significance. They do mean that this paper
cannot claim the mechanism improves held-out $F_1$: every comparison
favours max-merge or finds no difference. The rival ceiling
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}) offers one mechanism that can hold
back recall under the Noisy-Or rule but not under max-merge, although
it has not been shown to cause the ablation difference. The feedback
controller's ablation is a plain null ($-0.032$, 95\% CI
$[-0.090, 0.023]$): neither a benefit nor a cost of about $0.08$ $F_1$
or more is supported
% [EVID-045]
.

\subsection{Why Fine-Tuning Raised Precision but Lowered Recall}
\label{sec:discussion-finetuning}

This is consistent with how the fine-tuning data was built: the
synthetic training examples are generated from the knowledge base's
own high-confidence triples (Section~\ref{sec:related-work-dysect}'s
shared-capability description of this mechanism), i.e.\ from facts the
extractor had already found and the PKB had already accepted, not from
facts absent from its prior extractions. Training on this data can
teach the model to state already-discoverable facts more reliably and
suppress spurious alternatives -- raising precision -- but it supplies
no exposure to facts the extractor never found in the first place,
which would be needed to raise recall. In the earlier, 8-product
exploration (tuned on its own test set and superseded), this appeared
as precision gains with recall held exactly flat: at 5 epochs with seed 42, the fine-tuned model predicted 7
triples, all correct, and the base model predicted 46 triples, of
which the same 7 were correct (Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning}) --
the fine-tuned model's true positives were a subset of the base
model's, not a different or larger set.

DEC-027's leakage-free, 40-product replication
(Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning}) sharpens this account rather
than only confirming it: precision rose sharply in every seed
($P=0.704$--$0.894$ vs.\ base $0.568$), but recall now \emph{fell} in
every seed as well ($R=0.414$--$0.425$ vs.\ base $0.464$), rather than
staying flat. The same mechanism explains both patterns -- fine-tuning
on the PKB's own high-confidence subset narrows what the model states,
which suppresses false positives (raising precision) but can also
suppress some previously-recovered true positives that fall outside
the narrower, PKB-derived training distribution (lowering recall) --
and the larger, properly held-out test set was needed to detect the
recall cost at all: with 7 base-model true positives, a loss of about
10\% of true positives is less than one triple. The selection
procedure also favored this outcome: validation recall was flat across
configurations, so $F_1$-based selection chose on precision alone and
picked the most conservative adapter. The recall cost explains the
negative per-product bootstrap (Section~\ref{sec:results-finetuning}).
The positive pooled comparison comes mainly from false positives
removed on products where the base model had no correct triples, which
changes pooled precision but not those products' per-product $F_1$. These accounts are
consistent with the available per-seed detail but do not, on their
own, rule out other explanations for the same pattern.

\subsection{Why Calibration Is Poor}
\label{sec:discussion-calibration}

Two mechanisms are consistent with the poor calibration reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration} (ECE $=0.333$, zero correct
triples in the $0.6$--$0.7$ and $0.8$--$0.9$ bins). First, the
per-observation confidence entering Equation~\ref{eq:noisyor} is
self-reported by the extractor within a fixed, prompt-imposed range
($0.80$--$0.96$;
Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}/\ref{sec:results-calibration}),
not derived from token probabilities or any calibration procedure
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}) -- the input signal was never
designed to be a calibrated probability, so a poorly calibrated output
is an expected consequence of that input, not only of the aggregation
rule applied to it. Second, Proposition~\ref{prop:saturation}'s
saturation dynamic applies identically whether repeated observations of
a triple are correct or not: a repeated, incorrect extraction
accumulates confidence the same way a repeated, correct one does,
inflating scores on the same identical error rather than reflecting
genuine corroboration (Proposition~\ref{prop:independence}). This is
directly consistent with the two worst-calibrated bins being almost
entirely single-valued-predicate triples with a median of zero
competing values (Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration}) -- exactly the
uncontested case where nothing in Equation~\ref{eq:conflict} can ever
challenge a confidently repeated wrong answer. Both mechanisms are
offered as consistent explanations for the observed calibration curve;
neither was isolated experimentally from the other in this paper.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{A synthetic primary domain.} Every product-domain result
comes from a deterministic synthetic generator in which the structured
records, the text, and both gold sets are produced by the same function
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-datasets}). The domain names each fact
consistently across sources, is fully labelled, and has a fixed
13-predicate schema, which real product data would not. The two public
corpora (Sections~\ref{sec:results-docred}
and~\ref{sec:results-biored}) show how differently the aggregation stage
behaves on real text: on both, aggregation lowered $F_1$ relative to no
aggregation. The product-domain numbers should therefore be read as
results under favourable conditions, not as estimates for deployment.

\paragraph{The provenance gate was tested against a generator-derived
source.} The filter's rise in precision from $93.5\%$ to $100.0\%$ was
measured with a structured source generated from the same attribute
values as the gold triples (Section~\ref{sec:results-provenance}), so
the structured source was never wrong
% [EVID-029]
. DEC-028 partly mitigates this by corrupting the structured source at
5\%, 10\%, and 20\%: the filter's precision advantage decayed from
$+0.991$ to $+0.823$ (95\% CI $[0.688, 0.911]$) but remained
significant at every rate
% [EVID-042]
. That corruption was injected at random by this project, however, and
an independent real-world structured source may be wrong in correlated
ways that random corruption does not model.

\paragraph{The feedback loop is not self-supervised.} The feedback
controller builds its hint from the training-split gold labels: missing
facts and false patterns are computed against the known answers
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-feedback})
% [src/feedback_builder.py; src/experiment_runner.py]
. The closed loop as implemented presupposes labelled training data,
which exists here only because the domain is synthetic. A deployment
without gold labels would need a feedback signal derived from the
knowledge base itself, which was not built or tested.

\paragraph{Ablation power.} With thirty seeds the module ablation
detects effects of $0.070$--$0.086$ absolute $F_1$ with 80\% power
(Section~\ref{sec:results-ablation}). Smaller effects of the feedback
controller or the probabilistic knowledge base, in either direction,
are not ruled out
% [EVID-045]
.

\paragraph{The closed-loop test is a single seed with a model-family
confound.} The closed-loop integration test used one seed (43, the best
of three in the standalone evaluation) and compared a Llama-3.1-8B
control with a treatment arm fine-tuned from Mistral-7B, so any
difference between the arms cannot be attributed to fine-tuning alone
(Section~\ref{sec:results-closedloop})
% [EVID-030]
. It was not repeated across seeds or with a Llama-based adapter.

\paragraph{Self-reported confidence from two prompt templates.} The
extraction prompt asks the model for a confidence between $0.80$ and
$0.96$, and the fine-tuning data-generation and evaluation prompt for
one between $0.85$ and $0.99$
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-hyperparameters}). Neither value is derived
from token probabilities or calibrated, and the two ranges are not on a
common scale, so confidence values from the two paths are not
comparable. The aggregated score is poorly calibrated on real data (ECE
$=0.333$; Section~\ref{sec:results-calibration})
% [EVID-036; EVID-041]
.

\paragraph{Software versions.} No fine-tuning run in this project
pinned the versions of torch, transformers, trl, or bitsandbytes,
including the DEC-027 run that supplies the headline fine-tuning result.
Only the PEFT version can be recovered, from adapter metadata (0.21.0
for every DEC-027 adapter; 0.20.0 for the first DEC-006 adapter). A
record of every installed version is written for runs after DEC-027 only
% [EVID-044]
. The API-hosted extractors are fixed by model identifier alone, and
the provider could change the model served behind an identifier.

\paragraph{The scope of the repeat-counting correction.} R3 corrects
the counting of repeated observations from one source, so it acts only
when the extractor emits the same fact more than once from a single
source. On BioRED it raised the precision of Llama-3.1-8B's admitted
triples by $0.054$ (138 repeats) but had no measurable effect for
DeepSeek-V3.2 (6 repeats). With Llama-3.1-8B its $F_1$ was slightly
lower than R2's, because some discarded repeats were correct
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-biored} and~\ref{sec:discussion-r3})
% [EVID-041; EVID-046; EVID-047]
. Its benefit depends on the extractor, and it does not address the
scarcity of corroboration across sources.

\paragraph{Small admitted counts on DocRED.} On DocRED, R3's precision
gain rests on very few triples: 36 admitted and 9 correct with the exact
key. Its confidence interval is correspondingly wide ($+0.190$, 95\% CI
$[0.084, 0.315]$), and its $F_1$ did not differ from R2's
(Section~\ref{sec:results-docred})
% [EVID-046]
. More generally, both public-corpus runs used sentence-level
extraction without document context, a single fixed admission
threshold ($\tau=0.70$), and, on BioRED, co-mention as a proxy for
annotation-level corroboration.

\paragraph{Unmeasured resource use.} Memory use could not be measured
reliably, because every scale ran in a single process, and GPU
utilisation during fine-tuning was not recorded
(Section~\ref{sec:results-scalability})
% [EVID-023]
. The scalability claims cover runtime only.

\paragraph{An unindexed candidate scan.} The evaluated implementation
scans every previously accepted candidate on each new observation,
which is $O(N)$ per call and $O(N^2)$ cumulative
(Section~\ref{sec:formal-analysis-complexity}). An indexed variant with
the same aggregation mathematics was benchmarked but not integrated
into the pipeline
% [EVID-036]
. This does not limit the scales evaluated here, where API latency
dominates, but would at larger scale.

\paragraph{The weakest extractor on CaRB.} The pipeline's extractor,
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, scored $F_1=0.467$ on CaRB against $0.571$ for
DeepSeek-V3.2, and ranked last of five systems in the 30-sentence pilot
(Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks})
% [EVID-032; EVID-035]
. Every aggregation, filtering, and fine-tuning result in this paper
sits on top of that extractor. The public-corpus results show that
extractor recall, not the aggregation rule, is the binding constraint
on the framework's aggregation stage, so a stronger extractor could
change several of these results.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper evaluated a closed-loop extraction design in which a
language-model extractor populates a probabilistically scored knowledge
base that feeds back into extraction, a design and aggregation rule
attributed throughout to \citet{aminnaseri2026dysect}. Its four
contributions answer the four research questions. First, a formal
analysis of the published conservative Noisy-OR rule shows that any
contested slot of a single-valued predicate scores below
$1/(m+1) \le 0.5$ and can never be admitted at a threshold of $0.5$ or
more (RQ1). Second, this ceiling blocked every contested slot on two
real-data snapshots and under a structured source corrupted at up to
20\%, where the same rule without its conflict penalty admitted most of
them. Third, counting distinct sources rather than repeated
observations raised the precision of admitted facts on the product
domain, DocRED, and BioRED. It does so only when the extractor repeats a
fact within a single source, and it had no effect for an extractor that
rarely did (RQ2). Fourth, a controlled evaluation found no end-to-end
benefit from the loop's mechanisms. Removing the feedback controller or
the probabilistic knowledge base had no significant effect at thirty
seeds, leakage-free fine-tuning was negative on its pre-registered
primary metric (RQ3), and on DocRED and BioRED aggregation lowered $F_1$
relative to no aggregation for both extractors tested (RQ4). The binding
constraint on real text is extractor recall: the extractors recovered
fewer than 1.4\% of gold facts from more than one sentence, so an
aggregation rule that requires corroboration had almost nothing to
admit.

These results leave several questions open, and each could be settled
by a specific experiment:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Would aggregation help with a stronger extractor?}
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-docred} attributes the public-corpus failure
to extractor recall. A direct test is to repeat the DocRED and BioRED
runs with document-level extraction and stronger extractors, measure
the share of gold facts recovered from two or more sources for each,
and check whether aggregation's $F_1$ relative to no aggregation rises
as that share rises. If it does not, the constraint lies elsewhere.
\item \textbf{Can the loop work without gold labels?} The feedback
controller computes its hint against training-split gold labels
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-feedback}). Replacing that signal with one
derived from the knowledge base itself, such as disagreement or low
aggregated confidence, and repeating the thirty-seed ablation would show
whether a self-supervised version helps, hurts, or matches the current
null.
\item \textbf{Do the modules have small effects?} The ablation rules out
effects of about $0.08$ $F_1$ or larger. Detecting an effect of $0.04$
with 80\% power would need about 110--130 seeds at the observed
variance, which at the measured cost per run is under one US dollar of
API calls.
\item \textbf{Does fine-tuning help once its known confounds are
removed?} The closed-loop test compared a Mistral-based adapter with a
Llama-based control on one seed, and every fine-tuning run used
training data that had not passed the provenance filter. Repeating the
closed-loop test across seeds with an adapter on the control's own base
model, and the leakage-free evaluation with provenance-filtered data,
would separate the effect of fine-tuning from both confounds.
\item \textbf{Does the provenance gate hold against a real structured
source?} Its precision gain was measured against a generator-derived
source and random corruption. Testing it with an independently
produced structured source, whose errors may be correlated with the
text's, would show whether the gain survives realistic noise.
\item \textbf{Can the scores be calibrated?} The aggregated score is
poorly calibrated (ECE $=0.333$) and built from self-reported
confidences. Repeating the calibration measurement with confidences
derived from token probabilities would show how much of the
miscalibration comes from the input signal and how much from the
aggregation rule.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Taken together, the evidence supports the formal properties of
the aggregation rule and the narrow, scope-limited value of the
distinct-source correction. It does not support the claim that the
closed loop, as designed and implemented here, improves extraction.
